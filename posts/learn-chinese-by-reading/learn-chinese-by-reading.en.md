You can learn Chinese by reading, but Chinese changes the order of operations. In Spanish or French, the alphabet lets you sound out a page on day one, and the only wall is vocabulary. In Chinese, the page itself is part of the lesson: no spaces between words, characters that do not spell their own sound, and tones that change meaning.

The good news is that Chinese also ships with built-in help most languages do not have. Pinyin, the romanisation of Mandarin sounds, can sit above the characters until you no longer need it. A mature graded-reader shelf starts at 150 characters. And graded news rewrites the day's events at your level, so the jump to real-world text does not have to be a cliff.

## What makes Chinese reading different

Three differences decide everything else.

First, there are no spaces. Word boundaries are something you learn to see. A string like 中国人 is one word (Chinese person), not three separate ideas, and a beginner who reads character by character will never feel a sentence move. This is a skill reading builds faster than any drill: after a few hundred pages, your eyes chunk sentences on their own.

Second, characters carry meaning, not sound. The same character is read differently in different words, which is why memorising isolated readings before reading tends to disappoint. Reading shows you the character inside real words, repeatedly, which is the form in which you will actually meet it.

Third, tones ride on words, not on characters in isolation. The syllable ma can be mother, hemp, horse, or scold depending on tone, so a word you recognise by shape but cannot pronounce is only half learned. Silent reading still needs the tone attached to the word, or words blur together in memory.

One more difference is about counting, and it causes more confusion than the other three combined. Chinese levels are measured in three units that do not convert: HSK words, unique characters, and base words. Our [Chinese graded readers](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/chinese-graded-readers) guide explains the three systems side by side; here, the rule that matters is to treat each label as an address inside one series only.

## What you need before page one

Less than you think, but not nothing.

Learn pinyin until you can read it slowly without a chart. Pinyin is a small system, initials plus finals plus four tones, and it is the key that unlocks every annotated text below. Without it, you cannot use the training wheels at all; with it, you can start reading this week.

Pick up the most basic grammar as you go. Chinese sentence order is mostly subject, verb, object, the same shape as English, and the patterns that carry most beginner text are few: 的 to link a description to a noun, 了 to mark completion or change, and measure words such as 个 between a number and a noun. You do not need a course first. Ten minutes with a reference when a pattern keeps appearing beats a month of preparation, because the pattern is already in front of you in real sentences.

Do not wait until you "know enough characters". With annotated texts, character knowledge is an output of reading, not an entry ticket. Waiting reverses the causality and is the most common reason learners postpone reading for a year.

## Start with pinyin-annotated stories, then fade the pinyin

The best first texts are short stories with pinyin you can control. Du Chinese is the clearest example: its store listing describes material from HSK 1 to HSK 6, more than 3,000 readings, native-speaker audio, a built-in dictionary, and pinyin you can show for all words, for difficult words only, or hide entirely. That control is the whole method in one setting.

Fade it in stages, on a schedule, not when you feel ready:

1. Pinyin over every word, while you learn to match sound to shape.
2. Pinyin for new words only, so familiar words have to stand on their own characters.
3. Pinyin in the glossary only, consulted after you have tried the character.
4. No pinyin, with audio available when a sentence still will not resolve.

A scholar of Chinese, Victor Mair, once described a similar three-stage shape: learn the language first through transcription, then read large amounts of annotated character texts, then wean yourself off the annotations. The shape is right. The timing is negotiable: you do not need a year of transcription first when annotated stories exist, and the middle stage, not the first, is where most of the reading hours belong.

Pair audio with text in the early stages. Listen while you read, or listen to a sentence first and then read it. Tones land through the ears before they land through the eyes, and a word you have heard is a word you can keep.

## Learn to see words, not characters

This is the one skill the top search results for this topic mostly skip, and it deserves its own practice.

Take a sentence and mark the word boundaries before you look anything up. In 我喜欢吃中国菜, the words are 我 (I), 喜欢 (like), 吃 (eat), and 中国菜 (Chinese food). A reader that splits sentences into words, with tap-to-translate on each word, does this marking for you at first. Use one until your eyes do it unprompted.

Look words up as words. The dictionary entry for 东西 is "thing", and a character-by-character reading gives you "east west", which is correct and useless. When a lookup surprises you, the surprise is usually a word boundary you misjudged, and that misjudgment is the lesson.

Read aloud in short bursts. A paragraph read aloud forces tones back onto words and exposes the places where you were recognising shapes without sounds. Reread a favourite story instead of always pushing forward: the second pass is where chunking clicks, because the story no longer competes with the decoding.

## A ladder you can actually climb

**Step 1, annotated stories (weeks 1 to 8).** Read short annotated stories at HSK 1 to 2 level until finishing one feels easy. Rereading a favourite is progress, not cheating.

**Step 2, graded readers (months 2 to 6).** Climb one level at a time. Mandarin Companion is the usual entry: Breakthrough at 150 unique characters, Level 1 at 300, Level 2 at 450, with characters above the level underlined and glossed. Our [Chinese graded readers](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/chinese-graded-readers) guide compares the full shelf, from Chinese Breeze to the classical-novel sets, so this post does not repeat its lists.

**Step 3, graded news.** The Chairman's Bao rewrites current events at HSK 1 to 6+, publishing around 1,600 news-based lessons a year, with audio, vocabulary lists, and grammar notes per article. News is the first real-world text most learners can genuinely finish, because the topic is already half known from headlines elsewhere, and a daily article is a habit that survives busy weeks.

**Step 4, familiar novels and native text.** A novel you have already read in translation bridges to native material: the plot you know carries the language you do not. From there, native news, essays, and the graded classical sets in the companion guide are a matter of volume, not a new method.

The scale of the whole project is worth stating plainly. The US Foreign Service Institute puts Mandarin in its hardest category, at roughly 2,200 class hours to professional working proficiency. Reading with pinyin support and lookups a tap away brings simple texts within reach long before that, and every rung of this ladder is readable this month, not after the hours are done.

## Three traps on a Chinese page

First, pinyin dependence. Beginners often want pinyin over every line, and most graded readers refuse for a reason: your eyes take the easier path and the characters stop registering. The fading schedule above is the fix. If you have read fifty stories with full pinyin and still cannot read a line without it, the schedule slipped, not your ability.

Second, counting the wrong unit. A 300-character book and a 300-word book are different objects, because characters combine to form many words. Treat each publisher's label as valid only inside its own series, pick a sample with it, and let the sample decide.

Third, choosing a script late. Learn the script of the place and people you read for: simplified for mainland China and Singapore, traditional for Taiwan, Hong Kong, and much of the overseas shelf. Switching mid-ladder costs months of relearning shapes, so decide before you buy the second book, not the twentieth.

## How to read a page

Guess from context first, tap second, skip third. When a word blocks a sentence, guess its role from what surrounds it, then check. If you meet an unknown word every line or two, the text is above your level for now, whatever its cover says. One unknown word every page or so, most of them guessable from context, means the level fits.

Test a new book the same way before committing to it. Read its first pages for ten minutes: can you follow who did what, are unknown characters occasional rather than constant, and do you want the next page at the end? Labels narrow the choice. A sample settles it.

## Reading your own books

Lucerna does not supply books, and it does not sell a content library. It reads books you bring, with AI powered by your own key, so the method above applies to any book you already own once you can segment its words. That shape has one honest limit worth stating: no tool fixes a level mismatch. If a book is far above your level, the right move is still an easier book, not a stronger lookup.

Start this week with one annotated story, pinyin on, read twice. On the second pass, hide the pinyin for every word you already knew on the first. That single change is the whole method in miniature: support that fades as you improve, on a book you chose, at a level you can finish.
