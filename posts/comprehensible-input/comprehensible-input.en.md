You have probably met the advice in some form: stop drilling, start reading and listening to things you can understand, and the language will follow. The name for the material that advice points to is comprehensible input. The term is used loosely online, sometimes for any easy content at all. It has a narrower meaning, and the narrower meaning is the useful one when you choose what to read.

This is a concept guide. It explains where the term comes from, what the formula i+1 actually asks of a book, and how much of a page you need to know before the page can teach you. The practical method for building a reading habit lives in our guide to [learning a language by reading](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/how-to-learn-a-language-by-reading); this post stays with the idea underneath it.

---

## What comprehensible input means

Comprehensible input is language a learner can understand even though it contains some language they have not yet acquired. You follow the message: who did what, what happened next, what the speaker wants. A few words or forms are new, and the surrounding text, the situation, and what you already know carry you past them.

Stephen Krashen set out the idea in *Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition* (1982) and developed it in *The Input Hypothesis* (1985). In his account, learners acquire language by understanding messages, and the input does the teaching. Studying rules has a separate, smaller role in his model: learned knowledge can edit what you produce, but it is the understood input that builds the underlying ability.

Two conditions in his formulation are easy to miss. The input has to be understood, which sounds obvious and rules out a large amount of well meant exposure. And there has to be enough of it. A single understood page is an event. Hundreds of understood pages are a method.

## i+1: a little beyond what you know

Krashen wrote the target as **i+1**. The *i* is your current level. The *+1* is language a step beyond it: a new word inside a sentence you otherwise follow, a familiar verb in a tense you have met but not yet made your own.

The formula explains two common failures at once. Material at i+0, where everything is already known, reads smoothly and teaches little. Material far above your level, i+5 in effect, stops being input at all because the message never arrives. You cannot acquire from a page you cannot follow, however good the page is.

In practice i+1 is not a measurement you can take before buying a book. Nobody can point to the exact next structure your brain is ready for, and Krashen's own claim was that you do not need to: if the input is understood and plentiful, the next steps are supplied by the material itself, roughly in the order you are ready for them. For a reader, the working translation of i+1 is simpler. Pick a book where the story stays clear and the new language arrives in a thin stream instead of a flood.

## How much of a text do you need to know?

Reading research gives the formula a rough numerical edge, in the form of **coverage**: the share of the running words in a text that you already know.

- Batia Laufer's work in 1989 put the threshold for adequate academic reading comprehension at about **95% coverage**. At that level readers could follow a text, with effort and with support such as a dictionary.
- Hu and Nation (2000) tested readers at set coverage levels and found that nobody in their study read adequately at 80%, and only a minority managed at 90% or 95%. For unassisted reading for pleasure they put the figure at about **98% coverage**.

Ninety eight percent sounds severe until you convert it. On a 250 word page, 98% coverage means about five unknown words. At 95% it means twelve or thirteen, roughly one in every other sentence, which is why 95% feels like work and 98% can feel like reading. Graded readers exist to hold you near the top of that range: their controlled vocabulary is coverage, engineered before you open the book.

Coverage is a guide, not a gate. A familiar story, a subject you know well, or pictures and context can carry you through a text below these numbers, and an abstract text can defeat you above them. Use the figures to judge samples, which is where the next section starts.

## How to find input at your level

Start where levels are stated honestly. Graded readers publish their word counts and levels, so the first shelves of any language are the easiest input to find; our language guides, from French to Japanese to Chinese, all begin there for this reason.

After that, three sources stay comprehensible longer than their covers suggest:

- **A book you already know.** A novel you have read in your own language arrives with its plot pre understood. The known story does the work context is supposed to do, so the language can be a step harder. This is the same reason [rereading a book you already know](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/reread-a-book-you-know-foreign-language) works.
- **A series, in order.** The first book of a series teaches you its cast, setting and favourite words. By the third book, the author has become your easiest author, because so much of the vocabulary repeats.
- **Nonfiction on a subject you know.** Knowledge of the topic supplies meaning the language has not given you yet. A book about your own work or hobby can be comprehensible a full level before a novel on an unfamiliar world.

Whatever the source, test it the way the coverage figures suggest. Open a page in the middle and read it. If you can retell what happened and the unknown words are occasional, the book is input. If you are constructing the page word by word, it is a textbook exercise wearing a novel's clothes, and it belongs to a different hour of the day.

## How to use it while you read

Comprehensible input asks less of you than most study methods, and what it asks is mostly restraint.

Read for the message first. Follow the story and let unknown words pass when the sentence still makes sense without them. Many of them return, and a word met six times in a book is often yours by the end without ever being looked up.

Look up the blockers only: the word that keeps recurring, or the one a whole scene turns on. That balance is the subject of our guide to [whether you should look up every word when reading](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/should-you-look-up-every-word-when-reading). Each lookup is a small withdrawal from the flow that makes input work, so spend them where the meaning is really stuck.

Keep the volume up and the level honest. Input works by accumulation, which is why it pairs naturally with [extensive reading](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/intensive-vs-extensive-reading): large amounts of understood text, read for pleasure, at a pace you can sustain nightly. When a book turns into a struggle for three sessions in a row, stepping down a level is the method working as designed, not a retreat.

## What the idea does not claim, and where it is contested

Krashen's strong version says understood input is not just necessary but sufficient: acquisition follows from input alone. That stronger claim is the contested part. Researchers including Merrill Swain argued from the 1980s onward that producing language, speaking and writing and being pushed to be precise, does work that input cannot do, and later work added roles for interaction and for noticing new forms. Few researchers dispute that input is essential. The dispute is over whether it is everything.

For a reader the practical settlement is undramatic. Build your learning around large amounts of input you understand, because no rival method replaces it. Then treat speaking, writing, and occasional close study as supplements that cover what reading leaves out, instead of waiting for input to do every job alone.

## Questions about comprehensible input

### Is comprehensible input just easy content?

No. Content with nothing new in it is comprehensible but sits at i+0. The input that teaches sits a step above your level, understood as a whole while still containing language you have not acquired.

### How do I know if a book is at my level?

Read a page from the middle. If you can retell it and unknown words are occasional, around a handful per page, it fits. If you lose the thread of what is happening, it is above your level for now, whatever the cover says.

### Can I use comprehensible input as a complete beginner?

Yes, at the smallest scale. Beginner graded readers, picture supported stories, and texts written for the first few hundred words are input designed for i near zero. A novel written for native adults is not, however patient you are.

### Does watching count as comprehensible input?

It can, when you understand what is said. Subtitles in the language you are learning help tie sound to text; subtitles in your own language often let you follow the story while the new language washes past unprocessed. The test is the same as for a page: did the message arrive?

Coverage figures are study results, not guarantees for any single reader or text.*
