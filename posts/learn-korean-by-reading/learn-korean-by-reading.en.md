Korean is the hardest language on the U.S. Foreign Service Institute list that still gives you a fast start. About 82 million people speak it, and the Institute puts Korean in Category IV, its hardest tier, at roughly 88 weeks or 2,200 class hours for an English speaker to reach professional proficiency. It sits there with Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin and Japanese. Then you open the alphabet, and the hard part turns out to be somewhere else entirely.

Modern Korean is written almost entirely in **Hangul**, an alphabet created under King Sejong in 1443 and officially published in 1446. It has 24 basic letters, 14 consonants and 10 vowels. The scholars who presented the script in the 1446 *Hunminjeongeum Haerye* famously claimed a clever person could learn it in a morning and a slow one in ten days. For an adult learner, a few focused sessions over a week is a fair expectation. You will be slow at first, but you will be able to sound out anything. Hanja, the Chinese characters, still exist, but you will rarely meet them outside academic writing, some newspaper headlines and names.

Then the page does something English never does, and the same reader stalls. The grammar is glued onto the end of every word. The spelling hides how the word actually sounds. There are two words for the number one. And the ending of every sentence changes with who is talking to whom. None of this is hard in the way Chinese characters are hard. It is a handful of habits, and a reader who learns to spot them early stops tripping over them.

The route in this guide is the one that fits the language. Start with texts written for learners, learn to recognise the four things that make a Korean page look harder than it is, and pair reading with listening enough that the sounds of Korean become familiar.

## What a Korean page does differently

Four features of written Korean account for most of the shock in a first original book.

First come the words with grammar attached. Korean puts spaces between words, which is a relief after Japanese or Chinese. But a "word" between spaces, called an **eojeol** (어절), is often a word plus several grammatical pieces glued on. *학교에서* is 학교, school, plus 에서, at. *친구한테* is 친구, friend, plus 한테, to. *먹었어요* is 먹다, to eat, plus past tense plus a polite ending. Korean is agglutinative. It builds meaning by stacking suffixes. The particles do the work English does with word order. Learn the top ones first. They are 은/는, 이/가, 을/를, 에, 에서, 의, 도, 와/과, 하고, 한테. They appear in nearly every sentence, so practise them until you strip them off without thinking. Then learn the common endings as patterns. They are -아요/어요, -았어요/었어요, -고, -지만, -아서/어서, -(으)면. Each one you learn makes hundreds of verb forms readable.

Next come the sounds the spelling does not show. Final consonants shift depending on what follows, so 국물 (soup broth) is pronounced *gungmul*, 같이 (together) *gachi*, 읽다 (to read) *ikda*. 한국어 (Korean language) is pronounced roughly *han-gu-geo*, not *han-guk-eo*. This is one reason to read with audio from day one. Sound changes are learned by ear. A word read silently with the wrong sound is a word you will not recognise when you hear it.

Then come the two number systems. Korean counts with Sino-Korean numbers (일, 이, 삼) for dates, money, phone numbers and minutes, and with native Korean numbers (하나, 둘, 셋) for counting things, age and hours. The number one is 일 in 일요일, Sunday, and 하나 when you count apples. The two systems share no words past ten, and a page can use both in one paragraph. Learn which system a situation calls for, and the numbers stop looking like two different languages.

Last come the speech levels. Courses teach the polite 해요 style first (가요, 먹어요). Narrative in novels usually uses the plain written style (간다, 먹었다), while dialogue may be polite, formal or casual. Webtoons use casual speech, 반말, with dropped particles and slang. Expect a few weeks of adjustment each time you switch. A book's style is part of its level, and a reader who has only met polite Korean will find a webtoon slow going even when the words are easy.

One thing works in your favour. A large share of Korean vocabulary comes from Chinese. A commonly cited figure, based on the *Standard Korean Language Dictionary*, puts Sino-Korean words at about 57 percent of the vocabulary. These words are built from reusable syllables, each with a stable meaning. 학 means learning, in 학교, school, 학생, student, and 대학, university. 전 means electricity, in 전화, telephone, and 전기, electricity. 국 means country, in 한국, Korea, and 외국, foreign country. If you already speak Japanese or Chinese, you get a real head start. 도서관 (library) maps onto 図書館 and 圖書館, and many others line up the same way. You do not need to learn Hanja to benefit. Just pay attention when the same syllable shows up across words with related meanings.

## Start where the text is controlled

Your first Korean reading should be written or adapted for learners, at a stated level, with the grammar kept inside what you have already met. That is what graded readers are. Our guide to [Korean books for beginners](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/korean-books-for-beginners) sorts the shelf by kind, and [Korean graded readers](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/korean-graded-readers) shows how to test the level in ten minutes. [Short stories in Korean for beginners](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/short-stories-in-korean-for-beginners) covers free short texts and A2 to B1 collections instead. Pick a first book from one of those three guides.

Three series carry the first rungs well. *Easy Korean Reading for Beginners* from Talk To Me In Korean holds 30 everyday texts of about five sentences, with a vocabulary list per story, full English translations and free audio from the publisher's site, slow and natural speed. *Korean Stories for Language Learners* by Julie Damron and EunSun You, published by Tuttle in 2018, holds 42 traditional folktales, Korean and English on facing pages, with a Korean-English glossary, cultural notes and free native audio for all 42 on Tuttle's website. The *Darakwon Korean Readers* series retells classic Korean tales in modern Korean, spanning CEFR A1 to C2, with English glosses at the page bottom, a full translation and comprehension questions in the appendix, and free MP3s by professional voice actors through a QR code. All three are modern works, so bring your own copy and import it into Lucerna to read.

Free reading can carry the first rungs too. The TOPIK GUIDE Korean Graded Reader is a free online reader that needs no account. It holds 500 original texts in six levels the site maps roughly to TOPIK 1 through TOPIK 6, with tap-for-meaning, English revealed one sentence at a time, and built-in flashcards. Naver Webtoon, the big Korean platform, lets you read a chapter free each day on many series. Pick slice-of-life, school or romance stories first. They have short speech bubbles, everyday vocabulary, and pictures that carry the plot. Avoid fantasy and historical series at first, which bring dense, specialised vocabulary.

Above the learner shelf, the ladder runs in a familiar order. Short originals and books for young readers come first, then a book you already know in your own language, then originals chosen by interest. A familiar plot does real work in Korean, because so much of a page's difficulty is guessing what a half-understood sentence is trying to say. Knowing the story removes half the guessing. *어린 왕자*, The Little Prince, in the translation by Hwang Hyun-san published by Open Books in 2015, reads cleanly in Korean, and knowing the story frees attention for how Korean says it.

==A book you can almost read is the right book.==

## Use a ten-minute sample test

Before you commit to any book, sample it. This is a practical test, not a level assessment.

Read for ten minutes with the dictionary closed. Then ask three questions. Can you say who is involved and what happened? Can you retell the passage in two or three sentences, in any language? Do you want to turn the page?

If the events never arrived, the book is too hard today, whatever its cover claims. Go one rung down. If you followed everything and felt nothing, the book is wrong even though the level is fine. Change the book first. Korean makes this test matter more than most languages do, because a page can feel easy from the Hangul while one habit, a particle you skipped or an ending you misread, quietly steals the meaning. If a second sample from the middle of the book reads the same way, believe it.

Count unknown words on one page as a second check. A page of about 200 words with fewer than 10 unknown words means you know roughly 95 percent of it, and the book is a comfortable read. Ten to sixteen unknown words means about 92 percent known, still workable, and you will learn steadily. More than thirty means you know less than 85 percent, and the book will feel like dictionary work. Choose by the count. In Korean, count a word as known only if you recognise it with its endings, not just its stem. 먹었어요 counts as unknown if you know 먹다 but cannot yet strip the ending on sight.

Short pages need a second measure. Webtoon panels and news briefs do not fill 200 words, so count per thousand instead. Fewer than 50 unknown words per thousand is about 95 percent known, 50 to 80 is about 92 percent, and more than 150 is below 85 percent. Either count answers the same question, how much of this page do I already own.

The same number shows whether you are getting better. Keep a note of the known-word share for each book you finish. When a whole book stays above 95 percent, you have outgrown its level, and the next book will feel easier even if its label says B1 and the last one said A2. Labels lag behind your reading. Your known-word share moves with every book you finish, and that is the honest measure of a B1 to C1 path. It also chooses your next book for you. The right next book is the one whose sample lands in the same band, one rung up.

## Turn one paragraph into a reading session

Work through this short passage, written for this guide. It is not an excerpt from a recommended book and it is not an officially graded text:

> 민준은 아침에 책을 읽어요. 책은 한국에 대한 이야기예요. 민준은 거의 다 이해해요. 천천히 읽고, 같은 장을 다시 들어요.

Read for the action first. Minjun reads a book in the morning. The book is a story about Korea, and Minjun understands almost all of it. He reads slowly, and then he listens to the same chapter again.

Then look at the traps, because they are the standard Korean ones. *민준은* is Minjun plus the topic particle 은. *아침에* is in the morning. *읽어요* is the verb 읽다, to read, in its polite present shape. *대한* is about, from 대하다. *이야기예요* is a story, plus the polite copula. *거의 다* is almost all. *이해해요* is 이해하다, to understand. *천천히* is slowly. *같은 장을 다시 들어요* is the same chapter, again, listen, from the irregular verb 듣다, to listen, whose stem changes to 들어요. Reread the paragraph now. The second pass should feel noticeably lighter, because the decoding is done and what is left is Korean.

## Pair the page with sound

Korean spelling is regular enough that you can usually sound a word out from its letters. Audio teaches the sound itself, the consonant shifts at syllable boundaries, the stress in a long word, and the rhythm of a sentence whose words run together. A word read silently with the wrong sound is a word you will not recognise when you hear it.

==Listen after you read, text in front of you.==

Listen in three ways, and use a different one each pass. First read a chapter for the story, looking up only the words that block the plot. Then listen while following the text, and let the sounds land. Then listen a third time without the text, if the story feels easy enough, and notice how much you follow by ear alone. The third pass is the one that turns reading skill into listening skill, and it only works when the first two passes have already done the decoding.

Audio is easy to find. The Talk To Me In Korean stories and the Darakwon readers come with audio of the same text, and the Tuttle folktales carry free native audio for every story. For news, start with stories you already know from English news, and read broadcasters' articles alongside their video, so you can read and listen to the same story. Reading aloud helps too, in small doses. One paragraph, slowly, after you have heard it read properly, teaches your mouth where the consonant shifts actually sit. Do not turn every session into pronunciation drill. The point is calibration. A few minutes of sound per session keeps the rhythm in your head close to the language as it is spoken, instead of close to its spelling.

## What to do about unknown words

Look up a word when it blocks the action, when it keeps coming back, or when it looks familiar and the sentence still makes no sense. That third case is the Korean special, a familiar stem in an unfamiliar costume. *먹어요*, *먹었어요* and *먹을 거예요* are all 먹다, to eat. Save the dictionary form, not the shape you met, and keep the whole family on the same card, with every form you have seen, the original sentence, and the translation that fits that sentence.

==Save the lemma, not the form.==

A Korean word family is one item, not five. *먹어요*, *먹었어요*, *먹을 거예요*, *먹고* and *먹지* all belong to 먹다, and learning them as one card is the difference between a vocabulary list that grows and one that floods. Irregular verbs need the same treatment, with their changed stems written on the card. 들어요 comes from 듣다, to listen, 도와요 from 돕다, to help, and 몰라요 from 모르다, to not know. Nouns go on the card as the stem, with a note of the particles they travel with, *학교*, *학교에*, *학교에서*, not three separate cards. The fuller argument, and the habits that protect a story from its dictionary, are in [should you look up every word when reading](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/should-you-look-up-every-word-when-reading).

Save fixed phrases whole, too. *잘 부탁드립니다*, please take care of me, *어떻게 지내세요*, how are you, *마음에 들다*, to like something, to be to one's taste, and *잘 먹겠습니다*, I will eat well, are each one item, with their particles attached, not words to relearn separately. Learn pairs as contrasts as well. *은/는* and *이/가* both mark the subject of a sentence, and the split between topic and subject is the lesson. *에* and *에서* both translate as at, one for being somewhere and one for doing something there. *일* and *하나* are both one, one from the Sino-Korean system and one from the native system.

Keep your own dictionary. One card per word family, with your own example sentence, the book it came from, and a note in your own words. A word you saved in one book should be recognised in the next, and the next book is easier in proportion to how many of its words you already know. When a word you saved comes back in a new book, that is the review working.

## How much to read each day

Read a little, most days. Korean particles and verb endings become familiar through repetition at short intervals, and a nightly fifteen pages will beat a Sunday marathon that leaves you too tired to open the book on Monday. Fifteen to twenty minutes a day is enough. Finishing matters. A graded reader you finish gives you the whole arc of a Korean story once, endings included, and endings are where the endings and the speech levels stop being a table and start being a habit.

There is no native mobile app yet, so read in the mobile browser when you have spare minutes. A chapter on the train counts the same as a chapter at a desk. When a finished book felt comfortable, sample the next rung before you buy anything. The ladder only needs to be climbed one test at a time.

Do not buy three books at once. Buy one, sample it, finish it, and let the test choose the next. A shelf of unread Korean books teaches nothing; one finished book teaches the shape of the next.

## The bridge to real books

The long target is real literature. Hwang Sun-mi is the first horizon. *마당을 나온 암탉*, The Hen Who Dreamed She Could Fly, first published in 2000, is a children's novel about a hen that escapes her coop and raises a duckling. Short, everyday vocabulary, plain written-style narration with spoken dialogue, plus the film and the English translation to fall back on. It reads as plain Korean, and the farm words are the only new thing in it.

Hwang Hyun-san's translation of *어린 왕자*, The Little Prince, published by Open Books in 2015, is the second horizon. You know the story, and the chapters are short. It is real Korean. Speech levels shift in the dialogue and the philosophical passages get abstract, but the story carries you through both.

Sohn Won-pyung is the third. *아몬드*, Almond, first published in 2017, is about a teenage boy who cannot feel or recognise emotions. The narrator's flat, precise voice means short, clear sentences, and the book won the Changbi Prize for Young Adult Fiction. Read it after the first two horizons, when the plain style feels easy.

After those three, the contemporary shelf opens. *달러구트 꿈 백화점*, The Dallergut Dream Department Store, by Lee Miye, first published in 2020, is about a shop where people buy dreams while they sleep, in episodic chapters and modern everyday vocabulary. *불편한 편의점*, The Second Chance Convenience Store, by Kim Ho-yeon, first published in 2021, follows a homeless man on the night shift at a Seoul convenience store, one customer per chapter, so you hear many voices and speech levels. *82년생 김지영*, Kim Jiyoung, Born 1982, by Cho Nam-joo, first published in 2016, is the life of an ordinary Korean woman, short, plain and report-like. Han Kang is the last horizon. *채식주의자*, The Vegetarian, first published in 2007, won the Man Booker International Prize in 2016, and *소년이 온다*, Human Acts, first published in 2014, is about the 1980 Gwangju Uprising. She won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2024. Her prose is poetic and compressed, so save her for last. All six are modern works, so bring your own copy and import it. Do not start with them. Reach them by the test, one rung at a time.

## FAQ

### Do I need a textbook, or can I just read?

You need both, and the textbook comes first. A textbook gives you the grammar and the first 1,000 words in order. Graded readers give you those words in real sentences. Reading without a course means guessing the grammar from examples, which is slow. A course without reading means memorising words you never meet, which does not stick.

### I already speak Japanese or Chinese. Does that change anything?

It changes the start, not the method. You will read the first pages faster than an English speaker, because the Sino-Korean vocabulary overlaps heavily with the characters you already know. The traps change too. Hangul still needs its week, and Korean grammar, the particles, the endings and the verb-final word order, is its own system, nothing like Japanese or Chinese grammar. Use the head start, and run the ten-minute test honestly. A reader who leans on the characters usually picks a book one level too hard.

### Which Korean should I learn, standard Korean or a dialect?

Learn standard Korean first. The dialects are real, with their own intonation and vocabulary, and a drama set in Busan will sound different from a Seoul news broadcast. Standard Korean is what the graded readers, the news, and the books in this guide use. Save the dialects as a reward once your standard Korean is strong.

### When can I read Han Kang?

When a B2 book stays above 95% on the sampling test. Start with *마당을 나온 암탉*, then *아몬드*, then *채식주의자*. The poetic compression and the emotional vocabulary are the hard parts, not the grammar. By the time you reach her, the sentence length is the only new thing left.
