Most people choose their first foreign-language book the way they choose a New Year’s resolution: ambitiously, and a little out of spite. They reach for the classic they’ve always meant to read, hit a wall of unfamiliar grammar on page two, and conclude that they’re not ready. The book was the problem, not you.

## Pick something you already know

The single best first book is one whose story you already hold in your head — a novel you loved in translation, a children’s classic, a film you’ve seen a dozen times. When you know what happens next, you can spend your attention on the language instead of the plot. Familiarity is a free dictionary.

## Mind the difficulty, not the prestige

Aim for a book where you understand most of a page on the first pass. A few things tend to land in that range:

- Graded readers — books written deliberately for learners at a given level.
- Children’s and young-adult classics — simple syntax, concrete vocabulary, real stories.
- Genre fiction you enjoy — mysteries and romance reuse the same words often, which is exactly what you want.
- Short stories — the satisfaction of finishing arrives before the fatigue does.

Skip, for now, the dense literary novel, the poetry, and anything in a regional dialect or older register. Those are rewards, not entry points.

## The parallel-text trick

If you can, keep the translation within reach — not to read alongside every line, but to consult when a sentence refuses to give up its meaning. A glance at the other version unsticks you in seconds and gets you back into the story, which is the only place real learning happens.

> The best first book is not the most impressive one. It is the one you finish.

Finish one book — any book — and something changes. You stop being someone who is trying to read in the language and become someone who reads in it. The second book is always easier than the first.
