Learning a language by reading runs on material. You need books at your level, more books after those, and something short for the days when a book feels like too much. A good share of that material is free, legally, if you know where it lives.

This page collects the free resources we point readers to again and again, grouped by what you would use them for. Every entry was checked on 2026-10-18, and the page is updated as resources change. Free here means the core material costs nothing. Where a site also sells a premium tier, that is said out loud.

---

## Free books to read

**[Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/)** is the first shelf to know. It holds 79,635 free eBooks, mostly works whose U.S. copyright has expired, and it is strongest in English, French, and German, with real depth in Spanish, Italian, and Portuguese too. You can read online or download EPUB and Kindle files, with no account and no fees. The catch is level: these are originals, written for native readers, so beginners do better with a story they already know.

**[Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/)** takes a smaller slice of the same public-domain ground and produces it properly. Volunteers retypeset each book, proofread it against page scans, and give it a real cover. The catalogue is far smaller than Gutenberg's, but if a classic you want exists in both places, the Standard Ebooks edition is the pleasanter file to read.

**[Wikisource](https://www.wikisource.org/)** is the community-run library of the Wikimedia world. Its coverage is uneven in a useful way: alongside the big languages it carries texts in smaller ones that commercial publishers ignore. Texts are read on the site rather than downloaded as polished eBooks, which suits shorter works and reference reading.

**[Aozora Bunko](https://www.aozora.gr.jp/)** is Japan's public-domain library. It holds more than 17,000 works of Japanese literature whose copyright has expired, free to read online or download, no registration. Soseki, Akutagawa, and Dazai are all there in the original Japanese. That makes it a destination for advanced readers, or for anyone working through a famous story with support.

For finding free books language by language, including library routes, see our field guide: https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/where-to-find-free-books-in-any-language

## Graded and easy reading

**[Tadoku free books](https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/free-books/)** is where Japanese learners should start. The NPO behind the tadoku method posts graded books from Level Start (a handful of words, mostly pictures) through Level 5, free to read in the browser or download as PDFs. Many carry furigana, and some have audio. The ladder is gentle enough that a near-beginner can finish a first book this week.

**[Fabulang](https://www.fabulang.com/en/fr)** publishes free stories sorted by level, starting at A1. The French shelf is the one we send readers to most, because the French graded market is thin at the lowest levels. A short levelled story is a cheap test: read one at A2 and you will know whether a full A2 collection is worth buying.

**[Lingua.com](https://lingua.com/)** offers reading texts with comprehension exercises across many languages, labelled by level from novice to advanced. Its French reading section alone listed 41 free texts when we checked, and the same shape repeats for Spanish, German, Italian, and others. More texts sit behind a premium tier; the free set is large enough to be useful on its own.

## Free lessons and foundations

**[Boston English Center](https://bostonenglishcenter.com/free-english-materials)** gives away a complete A1 beginner course: 36 lessons of about an hour each, plus 6 progress tests, covering the situations a new arrival actually meets, from the doctor and the pharmacy to job interviews and paying bills. It was made for teachers, libraries, and community groups to use and share, and the page states plainly what makes it unusual: no registration, no payment. A self-learner can work through it just as well as a class.

Lingua.com, listed above, doubles as a foundations stop. Beside its reading texts it runs vocabulary builders and grammar exercises with automatic scoring, in the same languages. Ten minutes of drills before a reading session is a reasonable use of it.

## News and easy articles

**[NHK News Web Easy](https://www3.nhk.or.jp/news/easy/)** is Japan's national broadcaster rewriting its own news in easy Japanese. Articles are short, the vocabulary is limited, and furigana can be shown over the kanji. It is free, and it updates with the news cycle, so there is always a fresh five-minute read. For learners past the graded stage it is often the first native material that feels manageable, because the topics are current events you may already know from your own news.

News has one structural advantage over books: it ends. A finished article is a small complete win on a tired day, and the same story usually runs for several days, so yesterday's vocabulary comes back in today's follow-up.

## Reference and tools

**[Wiktionary](https://www.wiktionary.org/)** is the free multilingual dictionary maintained by the Wikimedia community. It earns its bookmark for one habit: checking a single word in the sentence where you met it, with its grammar and usage attached, instead of running whole paragraphs through a translator. Used that way it supports reading rather than replacing it.

How often to reach for it is a real question, and our answer is in the lookup guide: https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/should-you-look-up-every-word-when-reading

## Questions

### Are these really free, or free trials in disguise?

The core material in every entry is free to use without paying. Two entries also sell something on the side: Lingua.com keeps extra texts behind a premium tier, and the tadoku world sells printed graded series, while its posted free books remain free. Nothing on this page requires a credit card to start.

### I am a beginner. Where do I start?

With the graded section, not the classics. Read levelled material first: Tadoku for Japanese, Fabulang for French, Lingua.com for several other languages. Gutenberg and Aozora Bunko become useful once a graded book at your level feels easy, or earlier if you pick a story you already know well.

### Can I read these in my reader app?

Mostly, yes. Project Gutenberg, Standard Ebooks, and Aozora Bunko offer downloads (EPUB or text), and Tadoku books come as PDFs. In [Lucerna](https://lucernaread.com/landing/en), the reader this blog belongs to, you import the file and read it with your place, notes, and saved words carried across books. Lucerna supplies no books of its own, and its AI features use your own API key.
