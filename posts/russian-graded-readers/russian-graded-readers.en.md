Russian asks more of a beginner than most European languages do. The alphabet is new, the stress of a word is not marked in ordinary text, and the same word changes its ending depending on its job in the sentence. A page of real Russian can therefore feel much harder than its vocabulary suggests.

Graded readers are the controlled way in: books written or adapted for learners, with vocabulary, grammar and sentence length held to a stated level, so your first book in Russian can be a complete story instead of a page you abandon.

This guide explains how the Russian level labels work, which books sit where, and how to test a book before you buy it. Edition details were checked against publisher and bookseller pages on 26 October 2026, and the titles are starting points.

---

## What counts as a Russian graded reader?

A Russian graded reader is a text written or adapted for learners in which the vocabulary range and grammar are held to a level the publisher states. Support usually travels with it: a glossary, an English translation, comprehension questions, or audio.

Two near neighbours cause most of the confusion. A book for Russian schoolchildren assumes a native child's vocabulary, which grows in a different order from an adult learner's. And a textbook passage stops when its grammar point is done. A graded reader keeps going until the story ends, and that length is where the repetition does its work.

Russian has a thinner graded shelf than French or Spanish, and the levels on the covers are not all measured the same way.

---

## Two ways levels are counted, and the word "beginner" is doing too much work

Most Russian readers use the CEFR scale, from A1 (a few dozen words, short present-tense sentences) to B2 (connected argument, wider vocabulary). Some publishers add their own scale on top, and a few sell by word count instead. Treat each label as an address inside one series only, pick a first sample with it, then let the sample decide. The [Chinese graded readers](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/chinese-graded-readers) guide applies the same rule to HSK, character and word counts, which do not convert into each other either.

The other trap is the word "beginner" itself. A true A1 reader uses short sentences and a few hundred words. A collection mapped to A2 to B1 is a different object: it assumes you already read Cyrillic fluently and know the basic cases. Both are sold as books for beginners. Before you buy, check the CEFR band on the publisher's page, not the word on the cover.

## Four Russian page traps, and what to do about each

**The alphabet comes before the first book.** Russian uses the Cyrillic alphabet: 33 letters, several of which look like Latin letters but sound different. Do not start a graded reader until you can sound out Cyrillic slowly, letter by letter. The alphabet takes days, not months, and every graded reader below assumes it.

**Stress is invisible in normal Russian text.** In ordinary books, nothing marks which syllable carries the stress, and stress in Russian moves: the same word can stress a different syllable in a different case. A graded reader that prints accent marks, like the Lingualism books, is doing real work for you. Where your book has no marks, listen to the audio first and pencil the stress in yourself.

**Cases change the word you just learned.** Russian has six cases, so a noun you know in one form appears with a different ending when it is the object, the instrument, or the location. Do not try to memorise full case tables from reading. Learn to recognise the pattern instead: when a familiar word shows up with an unfamiliar ending, check its job in the sentence before you look it up.

**Aspect gives you each verb twice.** Most Russian verbs come in pairs, imperfective for ongoing or repeated action and perfective for a completed one. Graded readers introduce the pairs gradually, which is one reason to trust the level order rather than jumping to a harder book for a better story.

## A ten-minute sample test

Labels narrow the choice. A sample settles it. Read the first pages of a candidate book for ten minutes: can you follow who did what, are unknown words occasional rather than constant, and do you want the next page at the end?

If you meet an unknown word every line or two, the book is above your level for now, whatever its cover says. One unknown word every page or so, most of them guessable from context, means the level fits. If a whole chapter passes with nothing new, take the next rung up, or read this one quickly for volume and speed. MeloLingua's free stories and Lingualism's free samples make the test cost nothing.

## When should you try an original?

After the B2 rung, the gap to real Russian narrows but does not close. Familiar children's verse and short stories are the usual next step, and a story you already know in translation helps for the reason the [Korean graded readers](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/korean-graded-readers) guide gives for its own ladder: the plot carries you while recognition is still slow. Expect case endings and aspect pairs to stay hard longest, and keep one graded reader in rotation while you climb.

## Questions before you choose

### Which level should my first Russian graded reader be?

Start one step below the level you are studying. A reader at your study level leaves no room to build speed. For most learners that means a free A1 story first, then the A1 version of a ladder book.

### Is an A2 to B1 collection a beginner book?

It is a beginner-plus book. It assumes fluent Cyrillic decoding and basic cases. If you are still sounding out the alphabet, start with an A1 reader and come back to it.

### Do I need a book with accent marks?

At A1 and A2, yes, or audio you listen to alongside the text. Stress is not marked in ordinary Russian, so a book without marks or audio leaves you guessing at pronunciation.
