Portuguese is a friendly language to read, and a lonely one to shop for. English has whole graded-reader series with hundreds of titles. Portuguese has a thin shelf. A few learner collections, one free reading site, and a lot of children's books that adults are told to read instead.

A graded reader is a book written or rewritten for one level. The writer keeps to a fixed set of common words and a limited range of grammar, so a reader almost never meets three unknown words in one sentence. For Portuguese, one extra question comes first. Which Portuguese does the book teach? A graded reader teaches European Portuguese or Brazilian Portuguese, and the two differ in pronunciation, in the second person, and in everyday words. This guide shows how to test the level in ten minutes, what to read at each level, and how to move up without buying a second library.

---

## What "graded" means for Portuguese

Three things show up on Portuguese books, and they measure different things.

CEFR levels (A1 to C2) are the usual labels. A1 is a first book with short, concrete sentences. A2 adds everyday past tense and longer stories. B1 is the first level where a reader can follow a real plot without help on every page.

The variant is the second label, and for Portuguese it matters as much as the level. European Portuguese reduces unstressed vowels, so a written word can sound like half its letters. Brazilian Portuguese keeps the vowels clearer. Portugal says *tu* where Brazil says *você*. Portugal says *estou a falar* where Brazil says *estou falando*. Everyday words split too: *autocarro* in Portugal is *ônibus* in Brazil, *comboio* is *trem*. A graded reader that mixes the two without saying so teaches both badly. Check the introduction for the variant, and keep one variant for the first year.

Headword counts are the third system, and the most honest one. A series states how many different words it uses. The free reading site Verbeto states its counts plainly. Its A1 stories use the first thousand or so words of Brazilian Portuguese, A2 about two thousand, B1 three to four thousand. A book that states its headwords is making a checkable promise. A book that only prints a CEFR letter is asking you to trust it.

Portuguese is the fifth most spoken language in the world, with more than 260 million speakers, and the U.S. Foreign Service Institute puts it in its easiest tier, Category I, at roughly 600 class hours to professional proficiency. Those hours cover the whole language. A graded reader is how you spend the reading share of them without stalling on page one.

==A graded reader teaches one Portuguese. Check which one before you buy.==

## The ten-minute sampling test

Open the book at a random page, not the first one. Read about 200 words, roughly one page, and count the words you do not know. Count a word once, even if it appears twice.

- Fewer than 10 unknown words in 200 means you know about **95%** of the page. The book is at your level. Read it.
- 10 to 16 unknown means you know about **92%**. The book is a stretch, and a good stretch. Read it with a dictionary nearby.
- More than 30 unknown means you know less than **85%**. The book is too hard for now. Put it back and try one level down.

The same test tells you when to move up. When a whole book stays under 10 unknown words a page, you have outgrown its level. That number is the one to watch across books, the share of words you already know. A rising share means the next book will feel easier, even if its label says B1 and the last one said A2. Labels lag behind your reading; your known-word share moves with every book you finish.

==The page is the test. Ten minutes tells you more than the label.==

## The shelf, level by level

### A1: first pages

Start free. Verbeto offers 50 short Brazilian legends at A1, each about two minutes of listening, with the narration playing on the page and highlighting each word as it is spoken. *A Boiúna* and *A Iara* are stories every Brazilian child knows, retold in short sentences in the present tense. Every text is free to read and listen to, with no account.

For European Portuguese, Frazely's *Daniel in Portugal* is written for absolute beginners, in 11 short chapters about a language course in Portugal, with a full English translation and every sentence recorded by a human native speaker. It is the only A0-A1 reader on this shelf built for Portugal rather than Brazil.

### A2: first finished stories

*Short Stories in Brazilian Portuguese for Beginners*, by Olly Richards (Teach Yourself, 2019), is eight original stories mapped to A2-B1, across science fiction, crime, history and thriller, each with vocabulary lists and comprehension questions, and an audio version of the same text.

*Portuguese Short Stories for Beginners and Intermediate Learners* (Language Guru) covers the same ground with more support. Every story comes with a Portuguese-English vocabulary list, comprehension questions, and a full English translation. At 85 pages it is a short commitment.

Frazely covers the European side at A2 with *Paul's Diary* and *Coffee, Yoga & Taxes*, 44 chapters each, and Verbeto adds 10 more free A2 texts. Our [short stories guide](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/short-stories-in-portuguese-for-beginners) lists the story collections by level if you want stories before novels.

### B1: adult books, not school books

*Intermediate Portuguese Short Stories* (Lingo Mastery) is ten stories by a Brazilian author at B1, each with a summary and vocabulary list at the end. Know its gap going in. Idioms appear without explanation, so you will meet phrases like *dor de cotovelo*, literally elbow pain, meaning jealousy, and be left to work them out.

Verbeto's B1 shelf is free and wider, with 49 texts and a Brazilian history series in simple Portuguese about the slave trade, the quilombos and Zumbi dos Palmares, each read aloud. Frazely's *Detective Kara* (A2-B1, 51 chapters) closes the European gap at this level.

### B2: the bridge, not the first book

This is where Portuguese does something English graded-reader series rarely do. Verbeto's B2+ shelf gives you the classics as the authors wrote them. Lima Barreto, Artur de Azevedo and Machado de Assis appear in 96 texts, with the spelling brought up to date where the edition says so.

Three native books make good first bridges. *Quarto de Despejo: Diário de uma Favelada* (Carolina Maria de Jesus, 1960) is a diary in plain and direct prose, in short dated entries, so you can read three pages and stop without losing anything. *O meu pé de laranja lima* (José Mauro de Vasconcelos, 1968) is narrated by a child, which does most of the work. The sentences are simple and the vocabulary is plain. *O Alquimista* (Paulo Coelho, 1988) has short sentences and a linear narrative, and many readers already know the story in English, so they can read for how it is said.

The far goal is Machado de Assis. *O Alienista* (1882) is a novella, not a novel. That is why it belongs here. It is short enough to finish, and finishing something canonical does more for your confidence than starting something famous. *Dom Casmurro* (1899) comes after that, when a whole Machado story stays above 95%.

## Four traps on a Portuguese page

Graded readers control vocabulary, but they cannot remove what makes Portuguese Portuguese. Four features cause most of the trouble, at every level.

First comes the variant. A book that mixes European and Brazilian Portuguese without saying so will teach you both badly. Two words need a warning. *Rapariga* means girl in Portugal and is offensive in Brazil, and *bicha* means queue in Portugal and is offensive in Brazil. Check the variant on the cover or in the introduction before you start.

Second come two words for one English word, twice over. *Ser* and *estar* both translate as "to be". *Ser* covers lasting traits, *estar* covers states and places. *Por* and *para* both translate as "for". *Por* covers through, by and because of, while *para* covers destination and purpose. Graded readers keep both pairs in play from A1, which is good. Learn each pair as a contrast.

Third come the sounds. The nasal vowels *ão*, *ãe* and *õe* are single sounds, not a vowel plus an *n*. In European Portuguese the unstressed vowels shrink or vanish, so the written word and the spoken word can look like different words. Hear the page as well as read it, and the traps shrink.

Fourth come the Spanish false friends. If you already know Spanish, Portuguese looks like a discount version of it, but the resemblance stops at the surface. *Embaraçada* means embarrassed, while Spanish *embarazada* means pregnant. *Esquisito* means strange, while Spanish *exquisito* means exquisite. *Largo* means wide, while Spanish *largo* means long. Cognates help until they do not, and the ones that betray you are always the common words.

## Save the lemma, not the form

A Portuguese page is full of one word wearing many shapes. *Falo*, *falou* and *falando* are all *falar*, to speak. Save them as one word, not three. A noun goes in with its gender and its plural, *o livro, os livros*. A fixed expression goes in whole. *Dar certo* (to work out), *ter saudades* (to miss someone or something) and *fazer falta* (to be missed) each count as one thing to learn.

Keep one wordbook across all your books, not one per book. A word learned in a graded reader should be recognised when it turns up in a newspaper. If your wordbook is split by book, you will keep relearning the same words. If it follows the lemma, each book makes the next one easier, and the known-word share you measured in the sampling test keeps climbing.

==One wordbook for every book. A word met twice is a word starting to stick.==

## Read, then listen, then read again

One pass is not a routine. Listen in three ways, and use a different one each pass.

First, read a chapter for the story, looking up only the words that block the plot. Then listen while following the text, and let the sounds land. The nasal vowels, the reduced vowels in European Portuguese, the stress in a long word. Then listen a third time without the text, if the story feels easy enough, and notice how much you follow by ear alone.

Audio is easy to find at this level. Verbeto reads its texts aloud on the page, with each word highlighted as it is spoken. The Richards stories come with audio of the same text, and Frazely records every sentence with a human native speaker. For public-domain books, pair the text with a LibriVox recording of the same book where one exists.

## Start free, and do not buy these

The free start is real. Verbeto, with its legends, stories and classics, costs nothing and needs no account, and its 5,000-word book is free to download as a PDF and EPUB. LinguaCommons offers a free Portugal Portuguese guide with its own graded readers and a 423-card deck. Project Gutenberg's Portuguese shelf and LibriVox recordings cover the public-domain classics. Buy one book at a time, and only after the sampling test says it fits.

Do not buy a second graded-reader series before you finish the first. Do not buy exam books before you need the exam; the CAPLE levels run from A1 to C2, and past papers help when you are one level below the exam, not before. Do not buy a "fluent in 30 days" book. Portuguese takes longer than a month, and the books that promise otherwise are phrasebooks with a deadline on the cover. Do not buy only children's books. A children's book is written for a native child's world, and an adult reader finishes it with playground vocabulary. Mix one children's classic with one adult story collection, and let the adult book set the pace.

==Free pages first. Pay only after the test says the level fits.==

## FAQ

### Which variant should I learn first, European or Brazilian?

Learn the one you will use. Brazil has far more speakers and far more books, films and music, so Brazilian Portuguese is the default choice for most learners. European Portuguese is the right choice if you live in Portugal, work with Portuguese clients, or plan to take the CAPLE exams. Either way, pick one and keep it for the first year. Switching variants early doubles the work.

### Do I need a textbook, or can I just read graded readers?

You need both, and the textbook comes first. A textbook gives you the grammar and the first 1,000 words in order. Graded readers give you those words in real sentences. Reading without a course means guessing the grammar from examples, which is slow. A course without reading means memorising words you never meet, which does not stick. Our [Portuguese books guide](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/portuguese-books-for-beginners) sorts the course shelf by job.

### How do I know if a graded reader is my level?

Run the ten-minute test from the sampling section. Take one middle page, about 200 words, and count the words you do not know. Fewer than 10 means the book is readable. More than 30 means put it back and try a level down. A book you can already read teaches you more than a famous book you cannot.

### When can I read Machado de Assis?

When a B2 classic stays above 95% on the sampling test. Start with his short stories on Verbeto, then *O Alienista*, then *Dom Casmurro*. The nineteenth-century Portuguese will feel old from the first page. By the time you reach him, the old spelling is the only new thing left.
