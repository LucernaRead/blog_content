Search for LingQ alternatives and the first pages you meet are directories, not guides. They rank products by votes and list course apps next to reading tools, as if Duolingo and a reader did the same job. They do not. A course teaches lessons in order. A reader lets you open something real, look a word up where you stand, and keep reading. If you want the second thing, most of the names on those lists are noise, and the choice gets much smaller once you filter them out.

LingQ itself is the baseline to beat. It lets you import almost anything, articles, video captions, EPUB, PDF, DOCX, TXT or MOBI files, and turns each import into a lesson where you tap unknown words, save them with the phrase you found them in, and review them by spaced repetition. Premium costs $14.99 a month or $119.99 for a year paid upfront, with a 24 month web-only term at $8.99 a month. The free plan caps you at 20 saved words in total and 5 imported lessons, which is enough for an evening of testing and not enough to learn on. It covers more than 50 languages under one subscription, and it has proper phone apps with offline study on Premium. Its weaknesses are specific: it hands you a library and an importer and leaves finding level-matched material to you, the review queue piles up if you save too many words, and it gives you no speaking practice and no structured grammar.

## Start with what you read

Answer one question before you compare prices. What do you actually open most days?

If it is a book you chose, you need an import-first reader, one that takes your file and stays out of your way. If it is articles on the web, you need a reader that works wherever you already browse. If it is shows, you need subtitles you can pause and click, not a book reader at all. If it is a facing translation, because you are at the very beginning and a page of unknown words is exhausting, you need parallel text with audio, not a lookup tool. And if it is nothing yet, because everything you try is too hard, you do not need a better reader first. You need level-matched material, and our guide to [how to learn a language by reading](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/how-to-learn-a-language-by-reading) explains how to pick it.

Ask a second question too. Do you want every word tracked, with a count that climbs as you read, or do you want reading to stay reading? LingQ is built for the first reader. Some people love the count. Others find the queue becomes the work, and a tool that only saves the words you choose is kinder to the habit.

## The tools, by the job they do

Readlang is the closest like-for-like alternative to LingQ, and the cheapest paid one here. It is mainly a browser extension: install it and any web page in your target language becomes clickable, news, blogs, fan wikis, whatever you were already reading. Click a word, get a translation, save it for flashcard review. It also reads uploaded EPUB and text files, though not PDF, and it has no native phone app. Premium costs $6 a month or $48 a year. The free tier gives unlimited single-word translations and 10 phrase translations a day. If most of your reading is on the web rather than in novels, Readlang is the better tool, and it is not close.

Language Reactor is for the reader whose reading is really subtitles. It is a browser extension that puts dual subtitles on Netflix and YouTube, lets you pause on any word for a translation, and replays lines sentence by sentence. It also takes text you paste in. The core features are free and a Pro plan adds extras. If your language learning happens through shows you already watch, this is the tool shaped for it. It runs in a desktop browser, so it is not a mobile experience.

Beelinguapp does something none of the tools above does: it puts the two languages side by side, with a recorded voice reading along sentence by sentence. For a complete beginner, that format is gentler than selecting words one by one, because your own language is right there on the facing page. The US App Store lists $7.99 a month and $49.90 a year. The limit shows up as you improve: it is a closed library, so you read what Beelinguapp has, not the novel you wanted. If you want the parallel-text idea in book form rather than app form, our guide to [dual language books](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/dual-language-books) covers how to use them well.

A Kindle you already own is the cheapest path of all, because it adds no subscription. It is very good reading hardware with a dictionary built in, and its Vocabulary Builder keeps one list across your whole library. Two limits matter. Vocabulary Builder exists only on Kindle e-readers, not in the phone app, and its flashcards were retired in late 2024, with no official way to export your word list. If you read on the device and a dictionary lookup is all you need, you may not need to buy anything.

Two tools solve the level problem directly, by shaping the text itself. Eppika takes well-known books and rewrites them to your level, from A1 to B2, in fourteen languages, with more than 120 titles. On its website Premium costs $19.99 a month or $89.99 a year. The trade is plain: you are reading a version of the book, not the book the author wrote. Simply Fluent takes a different route to the same gap: short stories written to a level from the start, plus complete classic novels with only the old-fashioned language rewritten the way people write today. Everyone starts with a 30-day free trial, then it costs €7.99 a month or €59.99 a year. If recent famous titles matter to you, Eppika is the one. If you want whole books rather than rewrites, the modernized classics are the closer fit.

Lute is the tool for the tinkerer. It is free and open source, installed on your own computer, and you read your own texts in a browser. There is no mobile app and no library, and you set it up yourself. If you like owning the whole setup and paying nothing, that is a real answer. If you want to open an app and read, it is not.

Lucerna fills a slot none of the tools above occupies. It is a reader for books you bring yourself: it imports EPUB, PDF and TXT files and supplies no books of its own. Its AI features use your own API key, so provider charges may apply, and the desktop version also supports local models. That means there is no content-library subscription to rent. The cost of the AI is your provider's, and it varies with how much you use. A free guest can use 1 book and save 5 words, and a free account allows 5 books and 200 saved words. If you already know which book you want to read and you want the AI without renting a library, this is the shape that fits. If you want someone to hand you level-matched material, it is not, because the book choice stays yours.

## What free really buys

Free tiers are not the same thing, and the caps decide whether free is a trial or a way to learn.

| Tool | Free tier | Where it stops |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LingQ | Library reading open, 20 saved words, 5 imported lessons | Saving words, the core of the system |
| Readlang | Unlimited single-word translations, 10 phrase translations a day | Phrase lookups and flashcards beyond the daily allowance |
| Language Reactor | Core subtitle features | Pro extras |
| Kindle, if you own one | Dictionary lookup, whole library | No subscription at all, but no export and no phone-app word list |
| Lute | Everything, free and open source | Your time setting it up, and no mobile app |
| Lucerna | Guest 1 book and 5 saved words, account 5 books and 200 saved words | Books beyond the allowance, and AI use on your own key |
| Simply Fluent | 30-day trial | The trial ends, then €7.99 a month or €59.99 a year |

Read the table as a test. If you hit the cap in the first week, free was a trial, and you should judge the tool on its paid price, not on its free tier.

## What a year of reading costs

Monthly prices hide the shape of the cost. Over a year, LingQ costs $119.99, Readlang $48, Beelinguapp $49.90, Eppika $89.99 on its website, and Simply Fluent €59.99. A Kindle you own and Lute cost no subscription at all. Lucerna splits the cost differently: no content library to rent, and AI paid to your own provider by use, so a light reader and a heavy reader do not pay the same. Before you pay for a year anywhere, run the free tier or the trial past its cap. A tool that survives two weeks of your real reading is worth a year. A tool that only survives its marketing page is not.

## Match the weakness to the fix

LingQ's weaknesses point at the alternatives better than any feature list does. If finding level-matched material is the hard part, pick a graded shelf or a rewritten-to-level book first, and add a reader second. If the review queue piles up, pick a tool that saves only the words you choose, and mark words known honestly instead of paging through them. If speaking is the gap, no reader fixes it: add conversation exchanges or tutoring, which LingQ itself sells separately in points. If grammar in order is the gap, add a course or a grammar book alongside the reader. If subtitles are your real input, stop shopping for book readers and take Language Reactor. If both languages on screen is what gets you through a page, take Beelinguapp or a parallel-text book.

The honest point underneath all of this is that the tool matters less than whether you read. Forty pages a week in any of these beats four pages in the best one. Pick the one that keeps you from closing the book, run it for two weeks, and let the cap you hit tell you whether to pay.

## Questions before you start

### Is LingQ worth paying for?

If you already read and listen a lot in the language and you want one tool that imports almost anything, tracks every word, and keeps the review queue, yes. If you want lessons laid out for you, speaking practice, or material written to your level, the money buys the wrong thing, and a graded shelf plus a cheaper reader fits better.

### Can I use more than one tool?

Yes, and most readers eventually do. A common split is Readlang for the web, a book reader for novels, and Language Reactor for shows. The cost stays sane when each tool covers a different kind of reading instead of duplicating the same shelf.

### Do I need to pay anything at all?

Not to start. A Kindle you own, Lute, and the free tiers of Readlang and Language Reactor cover real reading without a subscription. Pay when a cap you actually hit blocks real reading, not when a pricing page suggests you should.

### Where does Lucerna fit among these?

It fits the reader who already knows which book to open and wants AI help, lookups, sentence analysis, saved words, without renting a content library. You bring a file you are authorised to use, the AI runs on your own key or a local model on desktop, and there is no library subscription in the price. If you want the tool to choose your next book, look at the graded shelves and rewritten books above instead.
