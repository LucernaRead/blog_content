# Read like a Parisian: 7 short French novels to start this summer

> A reading ladder from A2 to C1, all of them on Project Gutenberg for free, none of them long enough to ruin your June.

There is a long tradition of arriving at the beginning of summer with a small stack of books and a quiet plan to actually read them. In Paris, that stack lives on the parapet wall of the Seine quays, slowly cooked in the sun. In a café in the Marais, it shares the table with a sweating glass of pastis. On the RER home, it survives someone reading over your shoulder.

The version of this tradition for someone *learning* French is a little different. You aren't trying to finish a 600-page Goncourt winner in two weeks. You're trying to spend a few hundred sentences inside French prose — the rhythm of it, the way the verbs land, the small idioms that no flashcard ever explains. Short novels are the right shape for this. They end before your goodwill does.

Below is a ladder of seven titles, ordered roughly by difficulty. All of them are on [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/) in the original French, all of them short enough to finish in a fortnight at five pages a day, and all of them rewarding to come back to a decade later. Pick the one a notch above where you feel comfortable. The point is not to be impressed by what you read; the point is to read it.

## The ladder

### 1. *Le Petit Prince* — Antoine de Saint-Exupéry (A2)

The classic gateway. Saint-Exupéry wrote in short, declarative sentences with a vocabulary that repeats throughout — exactly the conditions under which an A2 brain stops translating and starts inferring. The chapters are two to four pages long, which means you can finish one before your coffee gets cold. Re-read it after a year in French and you'll find a different book underneath; that's part of the deal.

> *« S'il vous plaît… dessine-moi un mouton. »*

>

> ("Please... draw me a sheep.")

### 2. *Carmen* — Prosper Mérimée (B1)

The opera came later. Mérimée's novella is shorter, drier, and stranger than the version you might have seen — a Basque deserter narrates his obsession with a Romani woman in nineteenth-century Andalusia. The prose is journalistic, the chapters are brief, and the plot moves. A good first encounter with adult French fiction.

> *« J'avais toujours soupçonné les géographes de ne savoir ce qu'ils disent. »*

>

> ("I have always suspected geographers of not knowing what they are talking about.")

### 3. *Le Tour du monde en quatre-vingts jours* — Jules Verne (B1–B2)

Phileas Fogg bets twenty thousand pounds that he can circle the globe in eighty days, packs a valet, and leaves. Verne writes in a clean, modern register — almost no nineteenth-century rhetorical curlicues — and the chapters end on small cliffhangers, which is the closest a nineteenth-century novel comes to TikTok. Excellent for endurance training: by chapter ten, your eyes are no longer tired.

### 4. *Lettres de mon moulin* — Alphonse Daudet (B1–B2)

A collection of twenty-or-so short stories set in Provence, framed as letters from an abandoned windmill. Each piece is six to ten pages long, so you can read one before bed and not lose the thread of a longer book. Daudet's French is warm, slightly oral, and full of the sort of regional colour that translators struggle to carry over.

### 5. *Candide* — Voltaire (B2)

Voltaire's anti-everything novella is two centuries older than the rest of this list, but the language has aged surprisingly well: short paragraphs, mock-naive narration, jokes you can feel land even when you don't understand every word. The shape of the sentences will retune your ear for French rhetoric — concision, irony, the rhythmic comma. Plan on looking up about one word per paragraph in the first ten pages, then almost none.

> *« Tout est pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles. »*

>

> ("Everything is for the best in the best of all possible worlds.")

### 6. *La Symphonie pastorale* — André Gide (C1)

A Swiss pastor takes a blind orphan into his home. He keeps a diary. The diary is the novel. Gide writes the way Camus would later — neutral, observational, deeply moral — but with longer sentences and a more interior register. Under a hundred pages and absolutely worth re-reading aloud.

### 7. *Atala* — François-René de Chateaubriand (C1)

A romantic novella set on the banks of the Mississippi, narrated by an old Natchez Indian to a French traveller. Chateaubriand wrote some of the most ornate prose in the language — long sentences, baroque vocabulary, distant historical setting. If you want to test whether your French has reached the point where you can sit with difficulty and not give up, this is a good test. If it's still too much, come back in six months.

## A reading method that fits a summer

Five pages a day. That's roughly 1,500 words, which is roughly fifteen minutes for a fluent reader and forty-five for someone at B1. Five pages every day for a fortnight finishes anything on this list.

Three rules to keep yourself out of trouble:

- Look up a word **only** when not knowing it would change the meaning of the sentence. Most of the time you can keep reading.
- When you do look up a word, do it without leaving the page. The moment you open a separate dictionary tab, you've stopped reading and started studying — a different activity with a worse retention curve.
- Star (or save, or copy) any word you've looked up more than once. Those are the ones your brain wants. The others, you don't need yet.

This is the part of the loop where a good reader matters. If you're using a paper book and a phone dictionary, you'll do fine for the first three days and quit by day five. If you're reading on a device that lets you tap a word for a translation, an IPA, and a verb conjugation table — and adds the word to a vocab list silently — you'll still be reading on day fifteen. [Lucerna](https://lucernaread.com) was built around exactly that loop, with French as one of its three currently supported lookup languages alongside English and German. Pull any of the books above from Project Gutenberg straight into the reader and start.

## The tote bag, packed

There is no rule that says you have to pick from this list. There is no rule that says you have to read in French. There is a quieter rule, ignored at your peril, which is that summer ends faster than you remember and "I'll start tomorrow" is the way most reading plans end. Pick one book before you close this tab. Five pages tonight, in French, before you sleep.

À demain.
