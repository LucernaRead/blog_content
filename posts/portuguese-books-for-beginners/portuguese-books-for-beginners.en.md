Search for Portuguese books for beginners and you get two kinds of lists. One is a textbook list. The other is a children's classics list. A textbook is a course, and a book you read through is something else. Beginners need both, in this order: one course to study with, and reading material to actually read.

This guide sorts the shelf by job: pick one book per job. Before that, pick a side. Portuguese comes in two main variants, European and Brazilian, and the books below are marked for which one they teach. A course written for one variant still teaches real Portuguese, but its pronunciation, its second-person forms, and some of its vocabulary belong to the other side. Choose the variant that matches where you plan to use the language, and stay with it for the first year.

## Start with the kind, not a famous title

| Job | What it does | Start here |
|---|---|---|
| Main course | Teaches sounds, grammar and vocabulary in order | Complete Portuguese, or Ponto de Encontro |
| Grammar reference | Answers "why is it built like that" | Portuguese: An Essential Grammar |
| Grammar workbook | Drills the patterns until they stick | Practice Makes Perfect: Basic Portuguese |
| Vocabulary book | Builds the most common words fast | A Portuguese frequency dictionary |
| Free reading start | Tests what level you can actually read | The free texts in the reading section below |

One book per row is enough. Two main courses at once is the most common beginner mistake: you end up comparing textbooks instead of learning Portuguese.

## The main course: pick one, finish it

A main course is the spine of the first months. Two well documented options suit different learners.

**Complete Portuguese** (Manuela Cook, Teach Yourself, 2010, ISBN 9781444107678) is the self-study route. Its 24 thematic chapters teach both European and Brazilian Portuguese through everyday situations, with audio sold separately, and it takes you to level B2. The chapters move from introducing yourself and dealing with everyday situations to using the phone and talking about work.

**Ponto de Encontro: Portuguese as a World Language** (Clémence Jouët-Pastré and co-authors, Pearson, second edition 2013, 624 pages, ISBN 9780205782765) is the university route. It was the first Portuguese textbook that lets the teacher choose Brazilian or European Portuguese, and the second edition is updated for the 1990 spelling reform, the Acordo Ortográfico. It is a classroom book: heavier, more thorough, and best with a teacher or a study partner.

If you want the Routledge route instead, mind the variant label. **Colloquial Portuguese** (João Sampaio and Barbara McIntyre, Routledge, 2002, ISBN 9780415274418) teaches European Portuguese. **Colloquial Portuguese of Brazil** (Viviane Gontijo, Routledge, third edition 2017, ISBN 9780415743969) teaches Brazilian Portuguese and takes you to A1 to A2. The two books share a series name and a layout. They do not teach the same Portuguese.

## A grammar reference and a vocabulary book

Once a course is running, two supporting books do work the course does not.

**Portuguese: An Essential Grammar** (Amélia P. Hutchinson and Janet Lloyd, Routledge, second edition 2003, 262 pages, ISBN 0415308178) covers both European and Brazilian Portuguese and marks where they differ. It is a reference, not a course: short readable sections on the points that actually confuse learners, with authentic examples. Use it the way you would use a dictionary. Look up the pattern that confused you in today's lesson, then go back to reading. Reading it cover to cover as a first book is a slow way to start.

**Practice Makes Perfect: Basic Portuguese** (Sue Tyson-Ward, McGraw Hill, second edition 2019, 256 pages, ISBN 9781260455236) is the workbook that goes with it: more than 180 short exercises, each finishable in about twenty minutes, covering high-frequency vocabulary, ser versus estar, saber versus conhecer, verb tenses and sentence structure. It also flags Continental and Brazilian variants where they matter.

For vocabulary, a frequency dictionary beats a themed word list. MostUsedWords publishes Portuguese frequency dictionaries that list the most common words with example sentences, so the words you learn are the words you will actually meet on the page. Learn the top 1,000 first. They cover most of everyday text, and the rest of the list can wait until you are reading.

## The free reading start

Before buying a second book, find out what level you can actually read. The cheapest test costs nothing.

**Short Stories in Brazilian Portuguese for Beginners** (Olly Richards, Teach Yourself, 2019, 240 pages, ISBN 9781529302806) is the first real reading to buy. Eight stories across genres from science fiction to crime, mapped to A2 to B1, written for young adult and adult learners rather than children. Each story comes with vocabulary lists and comprehension questions, and there is an audio version of the same text.

The free start is older books. Machado de Assis died in 1908, so his novels are public domain, and Project Gutenberg carries Portuguese public-domain books. **Dom Casmurro** (1899), his best-known novel, is the one to aim at, not the one to start with: a jealous husband narrating his own marriage, in nineteenth-century Brazilian Portuguese. Start with his short stories instead. Interlinear Books offers free Machado texts with audio and an English line under each Portuguese line, which is the gentlest way into real prose.

Use the ten-minute test on any book before you commit to it. Open a middle page, count the words you do not know on one page of about 200 words. Fewer than 10 means you know about 95% of the page, and the book is readable. Between 10 and 16 means about 92%, readable with effort. More than 30 means below 85%, and the book will feel like a dictionary with a plot. Move up a level only when a whole book sits above 95%.

==Pick the book you can already read, not the book you wish you could read.==

## Four traps on the Portuguese page

Portuguese has four traps that catch beginners, and all four are visible on the first page.

**The variant trap.** European and Brazilian Portuguese differ in pronunciation, in the second person (tu with its verb forms in Portugal, você in Brazil), and in small habits such as the Brazilian gerund (estou falando) against the European estou a falar. A book that mixes the two without saying so will teach you both badly. Check the cover or the introduction for which variant it teaches, and keep one variant for the first year.

**The verb trap.** Portuguese verbs change shape a lot. Falo, falou and falando are one word, falar, in three forms. If you save each form as a separate word, your wordbook fills up with the same verb ten times over. Save the base form and the sentence you found it in. The section below says how.

**The Spanish trap.** If you already know Spanish, Portuguese looks like a discount version of it, but the resemblance stops at the surface. Embaraçada means embarrassed, while Spanish embarazada means pregnant. Esquisito means strange, while Spanish exquisito means exquisite. Cognates help until they do not, and the ones that betray you are always the common words.

**The sound trap.** Portuguese nasal vowels have no English equivalent: ão, ãe and õe are single sounds, not a vowel plus an n. And por versus para both translate as "for" in English and split the work differently in Portuguese. Neither trap blocks reading, but both explain why a page can look familiar and sound foreign at the same time.

## Store the lemma, not the form

Portuguese is a language where one word wears many shapes, so how you store words matters more than how many you store.

Save the base form of every verb: falo, falou, falando all go in as falar, with the sentence that showed you the form. Save nouns with their gender and plural: o livro, os livros. Save fixed expressions as one item. Dar certo (to work out), ter saudades (to miss someone or something) and fazer falta (to be missed) each count as one thing to learn.

Keep one wordbook across all your books. When the next book uses a word you saved from the last one, that is the moment it starts to stick. A wordbook that resets with every book teaches you the same words again and again and never shows you that you are improving.

==One wordbook for every book. The second time you meet a word is when you start to own it.==

## Read, then listen, then read again

Reading alone builds vocabulary. Reading with audio builds the sound of the language at the same time, and Portuguese spelling hides a lot of sound.

The Richards stories come with audio of the same text, which makes the loop easy: read a story once, listen to it while following the text, then read it again without the audio. The second read is always faster, and the words you looked up the first time are the ones you recognise the second time.

For public-domain books, the same loop works with free material. Project Gutenberg supplies the Portuguese text, and LibriVox records public-domain audiobooks, including Portuguese titles. Find a text and a recording of the same book, and run the same loop: read, listen while reading, read again.

## What not to buy yet

Hold off on four purchases until the first course is finished.

A second main course. Two courses teach the same ground in a different order, and switching mid-course costs more than it saves. Finish one, then decide if you need another.

An exam book. CAPLE preparation books, past papers included, are for A2 and above. The exam levels run A1/A2 as ACESSO and CIPLE, B1/B2 as DIPLE and DAPLE, and C1/C2 as DEPLE and DUPLE. Buy the past papers when you are one level below the exam, not before.

A "fluent in 30 days" book. Portuguese takes longer than a month, and the books that promise otherwise are phrasebooks with a deadline on the cover.

A shelf of children's books as your only reading. **O Meu Pé de Laranja Lima** (José Mauro de Vasconcelos, first published 1968) is a Brazilian children's classic and a good early read, and **O Pequeno Príncipe** works because you already know the story. But an adult who only reads children's books learns children's words. Mix one children's classic with one adult story collection, and let the adult book set the pace.

## FAQ

### Which variant should I learn first, European or Brazilian?

Learn the one you will use. Brazil has far more speakers and far more books, films and music, so Brazilian Portuguese is the default choice for most learners. European Portuguese is the right choice if you live in Portugal, work with Portuguese clients, or plan to take the CAPLE exams. Either way, pick one and keep it for the first year. Switching variants early doubles the work.

### Do I need a textbook, or can I just start reading?

You need both, and the textbook comes first. A textbook gives you the grammar and the first 1,000 words in order. Reading gives you those words in real sentences. Reading without a course means guessing the grammar from examples, which is slow. A course without reading means memorising words you never meet, which does not stick.

### How do I know if a book is my level?

Run the ten-minute test from the reading section: one middle page, about 200 words, count the words you do not know. Fewer than 10 means the book is readable. More than 30 means put it back and try a level down. A book you can already read teaches you more than a famous book you cannot.

### Can I read a real novel as a beginner?

Not yet, and that is normal. A real novel at A2 is a dictionary with a plot. Read the Richards stories first, then a children's classic, then short stories by Machado de Assis. **Dom Casmurro** is the novel to aim at. When you can read a whole Machado story above 95%, the novel is in reach.
