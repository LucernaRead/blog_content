Dutch is a friendly first foreign language to read, and a lonely one to shop for. English has whole graded-reader series with hundreds of titles. Dutch has a thin shelf, with a few learner collections, some adult simplified books, and a lot of children's books that adults are told to read instead.

A graded reader is a book written or rewritten for one level. The writer keeps to a fixed set of common words, called headwords, and a limited range of grammar, so a reader almost never meets three unknown words in one sentence. The level on the cover is a claim, not a measurement. This guide shows how to test that claim in ten minutes, what to read at each level, and how to move up without buying a second library.

---

## What "graded" means for Dutch

Three level systems show up on Dutch books, and they measure different things.

CEFR levels (A1 to C2) are the usual labels. A1 is a first book with short, concrete sentences. A2 adds everyday past tense and longer stories. B1 is the first level where a reader can follow a real plot without help on every page.

NT2 levels come from Dutch as a second language, the exam world. The Staatsexamen NT2 has two programmes. Programme I is B1, for work and vocational study, and Programme II is B2, for higher education and professional use. The exam is run by DUO for the Dutch government and tests reading, listening, writing and speaking. A book tied to an NT2 level is written for an adult who lives in the Netherlands.

Headword counts are the third system, and the most honest one. A series states how many different words it uses. Oxford Bookworms Starters, for English, use 250 headwords at A1. The number tells you the size of the vocabulary you are allowed to meet. A Dutch book that states its headwords is making a checkable promise. A book that only prints a CEFR letter is asking you to trust it.

==The label on the cover is a claim. The page itself is the evidence.==

## The ten-minute sampling test

Open the book at a random page, not the first one. Read about 200 words, roughly one page, and count the words you do not know. Count a word once, even if it appears twice.

- Fewer than 10 unknown words in 200 means you know about **95%** of the page. The book is at your level. Read it.
- 10 to 16 unknown means you know about **92%**. The book is a stretch, and a good stretch. Read it with a dictionary nearby.
- More than 30 unknown means you know less than **85%**. The book is too hard for now. Put it back and try one level down.

The same test tells you when to move up. When a whole book stays under 10 unknown words a page, you have outgrown its level. That number is the one to watch across books, the share of words you already know. A rising share means the next book will feel easier, even if its label says B1 and the last one said A2. Labels lag behind your reading; your known-word share moves with every book you finish.

## The shelf, level by level

### A1: first pages

Start free. Duostories offers more than 100 short Dutch story sets with audio, written for learners. DutchPod101 gives two free graded-reader PDFs, one Absolute Beginner and one Beginner, with more inside its paid programme. Both let you hear the page as you read it, which matters for Dutch sounds.

### A2: first finished stories

*Short Stories in Dutch for Beginners*, by Olly Richards (Teach Yourself, 2019), is eight original stories mapped to A2-B1, across science fiction, crime, history and thriller. It is the one Dutch learner collection with a clear level statement and a real plot in each story.

*Nieuw in Nederland*, by Max Koedood (Van Dale), is a graded novel built around a core of 1,500 essential Dutch words, according to the publisher, with an audiobook, aimed at conversational A2. *Learn Dutch with Victor* (A0-A2) and *Story Dutch* (A2-B1), short story textbooks with pictures and audio, cover the same ground for readers who want exercises with their stories.

### B1: adult books, not school books

This is where Dutch does something English graded-reader series rarely do. *Makkelijk lezen*, easy reading, is a Dutch publishing line of simplified books for adults, often tied to NT2 exam levels and sold through Dutch-language bookshops. The stories are written for grown-ups who are learning the language. If you read Dutch in the Netherlands, ask a bookshop for the *makkelijk lezen* shelf.

*The Routledge Intermediate Dutch Reader* (2013) is a different kind of B1 book, with twelve chapters of graded readings taken from contemporary Dutch newspapers and magazines, about 1,300 words a chapter, each with a vocabulary list, comprehension questions and exercises. It suits a reader who wants non-fiction and is ready for real Dutch topics at a controlled level.

### B2: the bridge, not the first book

*Max Havelaar* (1860), by Multatuli, is the usual far goal for Dutch learners, and it is a far goal for a reason. It is a real nineteenth-century novel, not a graded text. Read it after graded readers have carried you to B2, not instead of them. The U.S. Foreign Service Institute puts Dutch in its easiest tier, Category I, at roughly 600 to 750 class hours to professional proficiency, but those hours are for the whole language. A graded reader is how you spend the reading share of them without stalling on page one.

## Four traps on a Dutch page

Graded readers control vocabulary, but they cannot remove what makes Dutch Dutch. Four features cause most of the trouble, at every level.

First come *de* and *het*. Dutch has two words for "the", and which one a noun takes is mostly memory. *De man* is the man, *het boek* is the book. Learn the article with the noun, every time, as one item. Say *het boek*, not *boek*.

Second come separable verbs. *Opstaan* is to get up, but in a sentence it becomes *ik sta op*, with *op* sitting at the end. *Meenemen* is to take along, and it becomes *ik neem het boek mee*. When a sentence refuses to open, check the end first. The missing piece is often sitting there.

Third comes verb-second word order. In a main clause the conjugated verb takes the second position, whatever comes first. *Vandaag lees ik een boek* is today I read a book, with *lees* still second. In a subordinate clause the verb goes to the very end. Read for the verb's position first, and the sentence settles.

Fourth come sounds and false friends. The *g* and *ch* sounds, and the *ui* and *ij* vowels, have no English equivalent, so a word you read silently can sound nothing like its spelling suggests. And *actueel* means current, not actual, while *eventueel* means possibly, not eventually. Hear the page as well as read it, and the traps shrink.

## Save the lemma, not the form

A Dutch page is full of one word wearing many shapes. *Loop*, *loopt*, *liep*, *gelopen*, *de loop*, *lopend* are all *lopen*, to walk. Save them as one word, not six. A separable verb goes in whole, *opstaan* with its parts, not *sta* on one card and *op* on another. A noun goes in with its article and its plural, *het boek, boeken*.

Keep one wordbook across all your books, not one per book. A word learned in a graded reader should be recognised when it turns up in a newspaper. If your wordbook is split by book, you will keep relearning the same words. If it follows the lemma, each book makes the next one easier, and the known-word share you measured in the sampling test keeps climbing.

## Read, then listen, then read again

One pass is not a routine. Read a chapter first for the story, looking up only the words that block the plot. Then listen to it, following the text, and let the sounds land, the *g*, the *ui*, the stress in a long compound word. Then read it a second time, and notice the sentence patterns you missed on the first pass.

Audio is easy to find at this level. DutchPod101 readers come with audio inside the programme. *Nieuw in Nederland* has an audiobook. Public-domain Dutch books on Project Gutenberg and the DBNL digital library can be paired with a LibriVox recording where one exists. Listen after reading, not instead of it, and let the same chapter pass through your ears after your eyes, so the spelling and the sound become one word.

## Start free, and do not buy these

The free start is real. Duostories, the two DutchPod101 PDFs, Project Gutenberg's Dutch shelf and the DBNL library cost nothing, and they cover A1 into B1. Buy one book at a time, and only after the sampling test says it fits.

Do not buy a second graded-reader series before you finish the first. Do not buy NT2 exam books before you need the exam; Programme I is B1, and its reading paper allows a dictionary, so the exam is not the reason to buy early. Do not buy only children's books. A children's book is written for a native child's world, and an adult reader finishes it with playground vocabulary. Adult simplified books exist for Dutch. Use them.

## FAQ

### Is a graded reader a real book?

Yes. It is a complete story, written to be finished, not a textbook chapter. The vocabulary is controlled, but the story still has to hold your attention. If it does not, the book is the problem, not the level. Pick another one at the same level.

### Should I read children's books instead?

Children's books are real Dutch, and a known story helps, because you already know the plot, so the language is the only new thing. But they teach a child's vocabulary, and they are not graded. Use one known story as a bridge, then move to adult simplified books and learner collections, where the level is stated and the vocabulary is chosen for you.

### How many graded readers do I need before real books?

There is no fixed number. The sampling test decides. When a B1 graded reader stays under 10 unknown words a page for a whole book, try a *makkelijk lezen* title or a short real novel. When that stays under 10, try the next level. Two or three finished books per level is a common pace.

### What if no graded reader exists for the book I want?

Read it anyway, with a plan. Read the first chapter with the sampling test. If the unknown count is over 30 a page, read a graded reader first and come back. If it is 10 to 16, read it slowly, save the lemmas, and listen to the audio if one exists. The book you want to finish is worth more than the book at the perfect level.
