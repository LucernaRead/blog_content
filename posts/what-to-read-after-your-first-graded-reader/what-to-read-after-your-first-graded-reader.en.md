Finishing your first graded reader is the real milestone. You read a whole book in another language — not a textbook dialogue, but a complete story, from first page to last. That matters more than it may feel like on the day you close it.

The second book decides what happens next: reading either becomes a habit or stays a one-off achievement. There is no single correct next step. There are three honest paths: stay at the same level, climb one rung, or move sideways. The right one depends on how the first book actually felt — easy or effortful, gripping or dutiful.

---

## Path one: another book at the same level

The most underrated choice is also the simplest: read another book at exactly the same level. Volume builds fluency. The first book at a level is where you meet its vocabulary and its sentence patterns; the second and third books at that level are where those words stop being new and start being automatic.

That change is easy to miss because it feels like standing still. It is not. If the first book felt like work, the second at the same level will usually feel lighter. Sentences resolve faster. Pages turn sooner. You stop translating in your head and start following the story. That lightness is the progress, even though the level on the cover has not changed.

## Path two: one rung up the same ladder

If the first book felt comfortable rather than merely manageable, the ladder itself is designed for the next step: same series, next level. You keep the familiar format, the familiar way exercises and glossaries work, and only the language gets harder.

These ladders do not stop at B1. The Black Cat–Cideb series continue past it: the French (Lire et s'entraîner) and German (Lesen und Üben) series run A1–B2 in four levels, the Spanish (Leer y aprender) runs to C1, and the Italian (Imparare Leggendo) runs A2–C1. For Japanese, the ASK tadoku ladder runs Levels 0–4, and Taishukan reaches Level 5. There is a rung waiting in every direction.

Before committing, use the ten-minute sample test from the guides. Read a sample of the next level for ten minutes. If you follow the events but miss details — a motive here, a description there — the rung fits. If you lose the thread entirely, the rung is too high for now, and path one is the better buy.

## Path three: sideways — a different genre or format at a similar level

Sometimes the level is right and the book is wrong. A sideways move keeps the difficulty roughly where it is and changes everything else: the genre, the format, or the kind of text.

Graded series contain more non-fiction than many readers expect. Our German guide's A2 pick, for example, was a biography — Sisi — which shows that lives, places, and history live inside these series alongside fiction. A regional or cultural reader can do the same work. Or change genre instead: a mystery after a romance, a thriller after a classic adaptation, and familiar difficulty suddenly feels fresh again.

Short forms count too. A collection of short stories or a novella lets you finish things often, and finishing is its own motivation. Sideways moves refresh motivation without raising difficulty, which is often exactly what a second book needs to do.

## When is it originals time?

Two signs together, not one. First, graded books at your level feel easy — not easy on a good page, but easy across a whole book. Second, a sampled chapter of an original is followable with effort: you work, but you know what is happening.

The bridge that works best is an original whose story you already know. Knowing what happens frees your attention for how it is said, which is the whole difficulty of a first original. Our guide to that approach is here: https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/reread-a-book-you-know-foreign-language

Keep a graded reader in rotation while you test originals. Alternate if you like: the original for ambition, the graded reader for momentum on tired days. And keep the timelines honest — a first original takes far longer than a graded book, which is normal and not a verdict on your level: https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/how-long-to-read-a-novel-in-a-new-language

## Line up the next two or three books now

Decide the queue while motivation is high, not when you finish the next book and drift for a month. Line up two or three titles, in the order you expect to read them, so the next book is always a decision already made.

## Questions

### Should I reread my first graded reader?

You can, and rereading consolidates: words that were effortful become familiar, and sentences you decoded slowly read smoothly. But a fresh book at the same level usually teaches more, because it shows you the same core vocabulary in new sentences and new situations. Save rereading for a book you loved. Note that our rereading guide is about something different — using a story you already know as a bridge to originals: https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/reread-a-book-you-know-foreign-language

### How many graded readers do I need before originals?

There is no quota. Learners vary too much for any number to mean anything. Use the sample test instead of a count: graded books at your level feel easy, and a sampled original chapter is followable with effort. When both are true, try. When they are not, one more graded book is never wasted.

### Is staying at one level for months a problem?

No. Volume at a comfortable level is how fluency is built: repeated words, repeated patterns, rising speed. Months at one level, with real books being finished, is a working method, not a stall. Climb when samples from the level above start to feel easy — your reading will tell you before any schedule can.

### What if I hated my first graded reader?

Change the story or the genre before you change the method. A dull book is evidence about that book, not about graded reading — the level may have been right while the plot, the adaptation, or the topic was wrong for you. A mystery instead of a classic, a biography instead of a romance: the sideways move exists for exactly this case. For choosing better next time, see https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/how-to-choose-your-first-book-in-a-foreign-language
