# Reading with a translation open: help or crutch?

> Almost everyone learning to read in a new language tries it: the original in one hand, a translation in the other. Here is when that helps you, and when it quietly stops you from ever reading on your own.

There is a particular setup nearly every language learner arrives at on their own. The novel in the target language, open. A translation — on the next page, the next tab, the next device — open too. You read a paragraph in the original, glance at the translation to confirm you got it, and move on. It feels efficient. It feels honest. And it is one of the easiest ways to read a hundred pages without your reading ever getting better.

That doesn't mean a translation is the enemy. It means the *way* you use it decides whether it's a ladder or a leash.

## What the translation is actually doing

When you read a sentence and immediately check the translation, your brain learns a quiet lesson: *understanding will be provided.* So it stops doing the hard, productive thing — sitting with an unclear sentence, holding the ambiguity, guessing from context — because why bother, the answer is right there. You feel like you're reading in the target language. You're really reading in your own, with the original as decoration.

The skill you're trying to build is *tolerating not-quite-understanding and continuing anyway*. A translation that's always one glance away removes the exact pressure that builds it.

## When a translation helps

Used sparingly, a translation is genuinely useful:

- **To unstick a sentence you've already fought.** Try first. Read it twice, guess, move on if you can. If a sentence is genuinely load-bearing — the plot turns on it — and you've actually tried, *then* check. You'll remember it far better for having struggled first.
- **To check yourself at the end of a chapter, not the end of a sentence.** Read the whole chapter in the original, then skim the translation. Most of the time you'll find you understood more than you feared. That feedback is encouraging and harmless, because it comes *after* you did the work.
- **As a confidence floor for your very first book.** If a parallel text is the only thing that gets you to finish one book and prove you can, use it — then drop it for the second.

## When it becomes a crutch

The crutch pattern is specific and worth naming so you can catch yourself:

- You check the translation **per sentence**, before trying.
- You read the translation **first** and then "read" the original to match.
- You never feel the discomfort of not-understanding — which means you're never practising the thing that matters.

If any of those describe your reading, the translation isn't helping you read the language; it's helping you avoid it.

## A better default: shrink the lookup, not the language

The reason people reach for a full translation is usually one or two unknown words per sentence — not the grammar, just vocabulary. A whole parallel translation is a sledgehammer for that. What you actually want is to clear the one blocking word and stay inside the target-language sentence.

That's the move: instead of leaving the sentence to read someone else's version of it, resolve the single word that blocks you — its meaning, its pronunciation, its grammar — and keep reading the original. You stay in the language; you just remove the splinter. Do that and the per-sentence translation habit simply has no reason to exist.
