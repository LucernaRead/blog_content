One of the quiet luxuries of learning a language through reading is how little it costs. Centuries of literature have fallen into the public domain, and most of it is a search away — free, legal, and waiting on a shelf nobody charges admission to. The hard part isn’t finding books; it’s choosing among them.

## The public-domain giants

Start with the large, well-organized archives. They hold tens of thousands of titles across dozens of languages, in clean EPUB files you can carry anywhere:

- Project Gutenberg — the original and still the broadest, strong in English, French, German, and more.
- Wikisource — community-maintained, with good coverage of smaller languages.
- Standard Ebooks — fewer titles, but beautifully formatted public-domain classics.
- National digital libraries — many countries host their own literary archives worth a search in the local language.

## Easier on-ramps

Public-domain classics can run old and formal. For something gentler, look for graded readers, dual-language editions, and “news in easy [language]” sites that rewrite current events for learners. They give you the daily-reading habit without the nineteenth-century sentence structure.

## Bring your own book

Anything you already own as an EPUB counts, too. A book you bought, a story a friend sent, a fan translation — if it’s text in your target language, it’s practice. The point was never to read something rare. It was to read something, tonight, that you want to keep reading tomorrow.

> The library is already free. The only scarce resource is the fifteen minutes.
