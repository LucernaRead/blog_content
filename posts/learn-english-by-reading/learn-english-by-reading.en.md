English is the most widely learned language in the world, and reading is one of the best ways into it. Every page gives you vocabulary in context, grammar in real sentences and a feel for how the language moves.

But "just read more" fails a lot of English learners. The language has three traps that other languages mostly do not. Spelling does not match sound. Common verbs change meaning when a small word follows them. And the verbs you meet most often are the irregular ones.

This post is a method, not a book list: pick the right level, test any book in ten minutes, handle unknown words with a system, and pair reading with listening so the sound of English stops surprising you.

---

## What makes an English page different

Three things trip readers up. Knowing them in advance turns confusion into a plan.

First, spelling does not tell you the sound. *Though*, *through* and *thought* look alike and sound different. *Enough* and *tough* share a spelling pattern and not a sound. You cannot sound a new word out the way you can in Spanish or German. Reading alone will not teach you pronunciation, so listening belongs in this method from the start.

Second, phrasal verbs carry the meaning in the pair, not the verb. *Put up with* means tolerate. *Get on with* means continue, or get along with someone. *Look up* a word is not the same as *look up* at the sky. If you save only the verb, you save the wrong thing. Save the whole phrase as one item.

Third, the most frequent verbs are irregular. *Go*, *went*, *gone*. *Take*, *took*, *taken*. A page of ordinary English is full of forms that do not look like their dictionary entry. If every form becomes a separate word in your notebook, the notebook fills up with the same ten verbs wearing different clothes.

==The traps are predictable, so the method can be built around them.==

## Start where the text is controlled

Graded readers are books rewritten to a fixed vocabulary, one level at a time. They are the fastest way to build reading mileage without drowning.

Three series cover the whole ladder. The **Oxford Bookworms Library** runs seven levels, from Starter to Stage 6, roughly **A1 to C1**, with over **270 titles**. **Penguin Readers** runs eight levels, Starter to Level 7, up to **B2**, each title labelled with its CEFR level. Olly Richards's *Short Stories in English for Beginners* gives you eight stories at **A2–B1**, written with about the **1,000 most frequent words**.

Start one level below what your pride suggests. A book you finish teaches more than one you abandon on page twelve. When the level feels easy, move up. That is the whole ladder.

For free reading, **Project Gutenberg** holds nearly **78,000 free eBooks**, mostly classics whose copyright has expired. Treat it as the second step: classics are ungraded and their English is older. Read a graded reader first, then test a short classic with the ten-minute test below.

## The ten-minute test

Never trust a level label alone. Test every book before you commit to it.

Open the book at a random page and read for ten minutes. Could you follow the story without stopping every sentence? Could you retell the page in your own words?

Count the unknown words on one page. More than about five, and the book is a level too high. Drop down one level and test again. Fewer than five, and you can follow the story, and the book is right. This test takes ten minutes and saves you from weeks of grinding through the wrong book.

A book that passes the test is a book you can finish. Finishing is the skill that matters most at the start.

## What to do about unknown words

Do not look up every word: that turns reading into dictionary work, and the story dies. Look up a word when it blocks the sentence, or when it comes back a second time. Skip a word that appears once and does not matter. Guess from context first, then check.

When you do save a word, save the base form. *Went* goes in the notebook as **go**. *Took* goes in as **take**. One verb, one entry, whatever form you met on the page. The same rule covers whole phrases: *put up with* is one entry, not three words.

Keep one notebook across all your books. A word you learned in a graded reader should be recognised when it turns up in a novel, not learned a second time. Review with the original sentence attached, so the word comes back with its context instead of alone.

## Pair the page with sound

English spelling hides the sound, so reading without listening leaves half the language out. The fix is a simple loop: read first, then listen, then read again.

Read a chapter on the page, then listen to the same chapter as audio, then read it once more. The second read is always faster, because your ear now knows words your eye only guessed at. **LibriVox** offers free audiobooks of public-domain books, read by volunteers, which pairs well with the same text from Project Gutenberg. Many graded readers also come with audio.

Do this with short texts first. A chapter, a story, a single article. Listening to a whole novel before you can follow a chapter is the same mistake as reading a book a level too high.

==Read first, listen second, read again. The second read is where the sound sticks.==

## From graded readers to real books

The ladder has four rungs. Graded readers first, until a whole book feels comfortable. Then children's books in real, unsimplified English: Roald Dahl, Dr Seuss. Then young adult and modern fiction, where the sentences get longer but the story carries you. Then the classics.

Try this paragraph, written for a reader at A2–B1. It uses the traps on purpose:

> Maya put off her English homework for a week. On Sunday she picked up a short story and read it in one sitting. She came across five new words. She looked up two of them and let the other three go. The next morning she ran into one of them again, in a news article, and this time she knew it.

*Put off*, *picked up*, *came across*, *looked up*, *ran into*: five phrasal verbs, each one a single item to save. Read it once, then read it again out loud. If it felt easy, you are ready for the next rung.

The step from graded readers to real books is not a jump. It is the same ten-minute test, applied to a harder book, one rung at a time. ## FAQ

### Which level should I start at?

One below what you think. If you are not sure, start with a Starter or Stage 1 graded reader and test it. Moving up is fast when the level is right. Getting stuck is slow.

### Do I need to look up every word?

No. Look up words that block the sentence or come back again. Skip the rest. Our guide on [whether to look up every word when reading](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/should-you-look-up-every-word-when-reading) works through the trade-off in full.

### Can I learn English just by reading?

Reading builds vocabulary, grammar and a feel for the language, but it does not train speaking or writing on its own. Use what you read: retell the story, write a short summary, talk about it with someone. Reading supplies the material. Using it makes it yours.
