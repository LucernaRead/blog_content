Korean has a reputation for a friendly alphabet and an unfriendly jump. Hangul can be learned in a few days, and then real books still stop most beginners cold: the vocabulary is unrelated to European languages, and sentences run in the opposite order.

Graded readers close that gap in stages: books and reading collections written for learners, with vocabulary and grammar controlled level by level, so your first Korean book can be one you actually finish.

This guide explains how the Korean level systems work, where to start, and how to test a text before you spend money. The titles below are starting points checked against publisher and bookseller pages, not personal recommendations from reading every one.

---

## What counts as a Korean graded reader?

A Korean graded reader is a text written or adapted for learners, in which the vocabulary range, sentence length and grammar are held to a stated level. Support usually comes with it: glossaries, English translations, comprehension questions, or audio read by native speakers.

Two things are not graded readers, even when they look similar. A native children's picture book assumes a Korean child's vocabulary and everyday knowledge — a different difficulty, not a lower one. And a textbook dialogue is not a story: it ends where its grammar point ends, and never asks you to follow a character anywhere.

The Korean shelf, long thinner than the Japanese one, has filled in quickly: free levelled texts online, beginner books from the main Korean teaching publishers, and a story collection written to a European level. The ladder below climbs in that order.

---

## Three level systems, none of them interchangeable

Korean does not have one shared grading scale. Three systems appear on covers, and they do not convert into each other:

- **TOPIK levels 1–6** — the scale of the Test of Proficiency in Korean. TOPIK I covers Levels 1–2 (beginner), TOPIK II covers Levels 3–6 (intermediate to advanced). This is the closest thing Korean has to a common yardstick.
- **TTMIK levels 1–10** — Talk To Me In Korean's own curriculum scale. Its reading book is labelled Levels 1–3 and its reading app spans all 10; those numbers describe position inside TTMIK's courses, not TOPIK results.
- **CEFR labels (A1–C1)** — used only by some internationally published books. Olly Richards' Korean collection, below, is mapped to B1 and approaching B2. Most Korean-published readers carry no CEFR label at all.

Some publishers grade by word counts instead. The Easy Korean Reading series by Jeanie Kim and Jon Tae Lee builds each level from the most frequent words: Level 1 works with the first 150, Level 2 with 300, Level 3 with 450 and Level 4 with 600, according to the series description on its Amazon listing.

The practical unit is the sample, not the label. Use a label to choose a first sample, then let the sample decide — the same discipline the [Japanese graded readers](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/japanese-graded-readers) guide uses with tadoku levels.

## The ladder: five starting points

| Starting point | Level as stated | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| Korean Graded Readers (website) | Levels 1–6, checked against TOPIK 1–6 | Free levelled reading practice online |
| TTMIK, *Easy Korean Reading for Beginners* | TTMIK Levels 1–3 | 30 short everyday stories with audio |
| *Easy Korean Reading* series (Kim & Lee) | Levels built on 150–600 frequent words | Stories in three passes, with free audio and video |
| *Korean Stories for Language Learners* (Tuttle) | Beginner, rising in complexity | 42 folktales on facing pages |
| Richards, *Short Stories in Korean for Intermediate Learners* | B1, approaching B2 | 8 stories built on the 1,000 most frequent words |

**Start free.** The Korean Graded Readers website, run by Korean-language teacher Chanho Park, offers free reading practice at Levels 1–6, and states that its levelling system verifies every text against TOPIK Levels 1–6. Free samples are the cheapest way to find out which TOPIK level you can actually read before buying anything.

**The beginner book.** Talk To Me In Korean's *Easy Korean Reading for Beginners* collects 30 short stories about everyday topics — cooking, hospitals, convenience stores, company dinners — for learners at TTMIK Levels 1–3. Each story comes with a key vocabulary list, a full English translation, and slow-reading audio. On the TTMIK store, checked 13 October 2026, the paperback is US$16.99 and the ebook US$13.50.

**The word-count ladder.** The *Easy Korean Reading* series (Kim & Lee) prints each story three times: sentence by sentence with a low-profile English translation, then in 100% Korean, then as a full word list. A free YouTube video and MP3 accompany each story. Because its levels are cumulative word counts, it suits readers who think in vocabulary size rather than exam levels. One condition, stated on the listing: it is not for complete beginners — you need Hangul first.

**The folktale bridge.** *Korean Stories for Language Learners* by Julie Damron and Eunsun You (Tuttle Publishing, 2018; ISBN 9780804850032; 192 pages) presents 42 traditional folktales in Korean and English on facing pages, with cultural notes, exercises and glossaries, and free online audio by native speakers. The publisher says the stories increase in complexity through the book. It is a bridge rather than a strict ladder: there are no level labels, so sample it after a beginner book, not before one.

**The intermediate collection.** Olly Richards' *Short Stories in Korean for Intermediate Learners* (Teach Yourself / John Murray Press, 2020; ISBN 9781529303056) contains eight stories across science fiction, crime, history and thriller, controlled to the 1,000 most frequent words and mapped to B1, approaching B2, with a glossary, plot summaries and comprehension questions. This is the first rung that feels like reading for pleasure rather than studying with a story attached.

## Start with Hangul, then a ten-minute sample test

Every rung above assumes you can decode Hangul. That is a weekend-sized problem, not a month-sized one, but it is not optional: graded readers in Korean are printed in Korean, and romanisation disappears after the first textbook chapters.

Before buying a book, read a sample for ten minutes. Check three things: Can you follow who did what without a translation? Can you keep going without stopping on every line? Do you want the next page?

If you decode Hangul so slowly that the story evaporates, drill the script first — that is a script problem, not a level problem. If you follow the events but miss details, the level fits. If you lose the thread entirely, step down one rung; the free website above makes that experiment cost nothing.

## When should you try an original?

After the intermediate collection, bridge gradually. Webtoons are a natural next step: short episodes, everyday dialogue, and pictures carrying the scene. A story you already know in translation works for the same reason [rereading a book you already know](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/reread-a-book-you-know-foreign-language) works — the plot supports the language. And when a graded reader is finished, the question of what comes next is the same in every language; the hub guide on [what to read after your first graded reader](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/what-to-read-after-your-first-graded-reader) lays out the three honest paths.

## Questions before you choose

### Do I need to know Hangul before starting a graded reader?

Yes. Learn to decode Hangul first — it takes days, not months — because Korean graded readers are printed in Korean without romanisation.

### Is TTMIK Level 3 the same as TOPIK 3?

No. TTMIK levels describe position inside TTMIK's own 10-level curriculum. TOPIK levels belong to the national proficiency test. Treat them as separate scales and trust samples over labels.

### Are there really free Korean graded readers?

Yes. The Korean Graded Readers website publishes free reading practice at Levels 1–6, with its texts checked against TOPIK levels, before any premium unlock.
