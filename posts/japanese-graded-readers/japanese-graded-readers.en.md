Japanese adds a wall that many other languages do not have: the writing system itself. You can finish textbook dialogues and still stall in front of a real book, because every page asks you to handle kana, kanji, and unfamiliar vocabulary at once.

Graded readers built for tadoku — 多読, “read a lot” — dismantle that wall in stages. They offer real stories from a few hundred words up, with controlled language and furigana carrying you over kanji you have not learned yet.

This guide explains how the levels work, where to start, and how to test a book before you spend money. The series below are starting points, not personal recommendations from reading every title.

---

## What is a Japanese graded reader?

A Japanese graded reader is a book written or adapted for learners. Vocabulary and grammar are controlled for each level, kanji carry furigana — small kana printed above them giving the reading — and pictures support the story. Audio is available for all titles in the tadoku series described here.

These readers follow shared principles under the stewardship of the NPO Tadoku Supporters. Tadoku's four rules are: start from a level you can enjoy; do not use a dictionary while reading; skip words or passages you cannot guess; and if a book stops being fun, quit it and pick another.

A native children's book is not the same thing. It assumes a native child's vocabulary and everyday knowledge, and is often written mostly in kana, which can make word boundaries harder, not easier, to see.

## Tadoku levels are not CEFR levels

Tadoku levels are the NPO's own scale, Levels 0–5. They are not CEFR levels, and they should not be presented as official equivalents to anything else.

For a rough orientation, the US distributor Cheng & Tsui publishes an approximate JLPT crosswalk for the ASK series. It is a distributor's crosswalk, not an official equivalence:

- Level 0 ≈ JLPT N5, about 350 vocabulary words
- Level 1 ≈ JLPT N4–N5, texts of roughly 400–1,500 characters
- Level 2 ≈ JLPT N4, 1,500–2,500 characters and about 500 new words
- Level 3 ≈ JLPT N3–N4, 2,500–5,000 characters and about 800 new words
- Level 4 ≈ JLPT N3–N2, 5,000–10,000 characters and about 1,300 new words

See the distributor's table on the [Cheng & Tsui Japanese graded readers page](https://www.cheng-tsui.com/browse/japanese-graded-readers) and the NPO's overview on its [graded readers page](https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/graded-readers-en/).

The practical unit is the sample, not the label. Use the crosswalk to choose a first sample, then let the sample decide.

## Use a ten-minute sample test

Before buying a pack, read a sample for ten minutes. With furigana helping you over kanji, you should follow who did what without a dictionary — tadoku says to put the dictionary away while reading, and to guess, skip, and continue.

Check three things: Can you follow the main action? Can you keep going without stopping on every line? Do you want the next page?

If you decode kana so slowly that the story evaporates, drill kana first. That is a script problem, not a level problem. Level 0 assumes you can read hiragana, with katakana learned soon after; kanji carry furigana, so you do not need to know kanji in advance.

## Turn one short passage into a reading session

The passage below was written for this guide. In plain text, this guide writes furigana as the reading in parentheses after a kanji:

> ゆうきさんは 駅(えき)で 電車(でんしゃ)を 待(ま)っていました。電車(でんしゃ)が 来(き)て、ゆうきさんは 笑(わら)いました。今日(きょう)は 友達(ともだち)に 会(あ)える 日(ひ)です。

Read first for the action. The parentheses show furigana's job: they print the reading over — here, after — a kanji, so the sentence can keep moving. 駅(えき) means “station,” and 電車(でんしゃ) means “train.” Those two nouns place the scene.

Then notice ました. This polite past ending appears in 待っていました and 笑いました, and it is the story's engine: waiting happened, then smiling happened. Read the passage again and let the known pieces carry the rest.

## When should you try an original?

After the top tadoku levels, bridge gradually. Manga or light novels you already know in translation can work well, because a familiar plot supports unfamiliar language — the same reason [rereading a book you already know](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/reread-a-book-you-know-foreign-language) is a useful bridge.

Native children's books are a different difficulty, not a lower one. For contrast, French graded readers follow a CEFR-labelled ladder instead; see [French graded readers for A1–B1](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/french-graded-readers-a1-a2-b1).

## Questions before you choose

### Can I start at Level 0 knowing only hiragana?

Yes — that is what Level 0 is designed for. Learn katakana alongside it, and rely on furigana for kanji.

### Is Level 2 the same as JLPT N4?

No. The Cheng & Tsui crosswalk is a distributor's approximation. Tadoku levels are the NPO's own scale.

### Are the free books really free?

Yes. Web Tadoku Books are published free as PDFs by the NPO itself; start from the NPO's [graded readers page](https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/graded-readers-en/).
