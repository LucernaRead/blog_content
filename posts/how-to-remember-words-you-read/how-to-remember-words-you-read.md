There's a particular frustration in language learning: you look a word up, nod, and meet it again three pages later as a total stranger. The standard fix is flashcards — drill the word until it sticks. But there's an older, quieter method that costs nothing and feels like reading, because it is reading.

## Why context beats drilling

A flashcard teaches a word in isolation: one prompt, one answer, stripped of everything that makes language meaningful. Reading teaches the same word wrapped in a sentence, a tone, a situation. Each encounter adds a thread — who said it, how it felt, what came next — and a word held by a dozen threads is far harder to forget than one balanced on a single definition.

## The power of meeting a word again

Memory runs on repetition spaced over time, and reading delivers exactly that for free. The words you most need are also the words you'll meet most often, so the language quietly drills you on its own most useful vocabulary — no deck to maintain, no review queue piling up while you sleep.

> You don't memorize the words you read. You befriend them, one encounter at a time.

## What to do with the stubborn ones

Some words refuse to stick — usually the ones that matter to you but stay rare in your reading. For those, a light touch helps:

- Jot it down, don't drill it. A short running list you glance at occasionally beats an anxious flashcard queue.
- Read more where it lives. If a word matters to your life, read about that subject — you'll meet it on every page.
- Let frequency decide. If a word never comes back on its own, it probably isn't worth the effort yet.

The goal was never a perfect memory for every word. It was to read enough that the words you need keep showing up — until, one ordinary evening, you realize you understood the sentence without trying to.
