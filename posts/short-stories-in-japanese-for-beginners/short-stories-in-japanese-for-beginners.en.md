A novel asks for weeks. A story asks for one sitting. That is why short stories are a fast first test of whether you can actually read Japanese yet. You find out fast, and a finished story is a result you can build on.

"Beginner" on its own tells you nothing. Every list calls itself beginner-friendly. Anchor it instead: if you know hiragana and katakana and a few hundred words, your first stories should run a few hundred characters, with furigana on the kanji and a translation you can check. When those feel easy, move up. That ladder, not the word "beginner", is what keeps you from opening a text that quietly defeats you.

## What makes a story a beginner story

Three things, and you can check all three before you read a word.

Length first. A first story should end in one sitting, ten to twenty minutes. If a story runs to chapters, it is a small novel, and it belongs on a different shelf.

Support second. At this level you need furigana you can switch off, a translation you can check sentence by sentence, and audio if you can get it. A story with no audio anywhere leaves you guessing at how the words sound, and the page cannot tell you.

An ending third. A story with a complete arc, a joke that lands, a folktale with a point, gives you something to retell. Retelling is the test that you understood it. A passage that just stops does not.

For Japanese, the ladder has numbers on it. The free Tadoku readers run Level 0 to 5. Level 0 and 1 use about 350 words, roughly JLPT N5, and Level 2 uses about 500 words, roughly N4. Start at Level 0 or 1. The level scale counts words, not pages, so trust the number, not the thickness of the book.

==A story you can finish is worth more than a book you can only start.==

## Free stories to start with tonight

You can read your first Japanese story tonight without paying anything.

- Tadoku free readers (tadoku.org). The non-profit NPO Tadoku Supporters publishes free graded readers, Level 0 to 5, most with furigana, many with audio, free to read online or download as PDF. Start at Level 0 or 1 and read several per level; the lowest levels feel too easy by design.
- NHK easy news is NHK News Web Easy, now called NHK Yasashii Kotoba News. It rewrites real news in simple Japanese with furigana and audio, free. One article a day is a story-sized read, and the news changes, so you never run out.
- Linguin offers free A1 to A2 stories with the English translation under every line, native audio, and tap-to-translate. The page itself gives you three stories to try in one evening. The Grateful Crane is a folk tale, Sushi Restaurant is a family scene, and Bullet Train to Kyoto is a trip.
- Hai Japan's nine stories are very short, with romaji and an English translation, plus audio files and a grammar PDF. Romaji is a crutch. Use it for the first two stories, then read the kana. Every week on romaji is a week not reading Japanese script.

If a story on any of these feels too hard, drop back one level instead of dropping reading. Our [Japanese graded readers](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/japanese-graded-readers) post explains the Tadoku levels in full.

## The first collection: pick one, not three

Two anthologies show the whole trap of this shelf. Compare them before you pay.

**50 Japanese Short Stories for Beginners**, by the Yokahama English Japanese Language & Teachers Club. It has 50 stories written in hiragana, katakana, and about 250 basic kanji, with furigana and a paragraph-by-paragraph English translation, plus a reading guide. As an ebook it costs 30.49 AUD. The catch is the content, since many stories are generic everyday scenes. The list includes *A Japanese in Munich* and *Americans in Germany*, so the book is fine for practice and thin on culture.

**Short Stories in Japanese for Intermediate Learners**, by Olly Richards (Teach Yourself, 2022). It has 8 stories, mapped to B1 approaching B2, with furigana on harder kanji, a glossary, comprehension questions, and audio sold separately. This is the next shelf, not the first one. The series has no Japanese beginner volume at all, so buying this book first is a common expensive mistake on this shelf.

One app option for readers who want a subscription instead of a book is Satori Reader. It costs about $9 a month or $90 a year, with the first month free, and the first two episodes of every series are free. Its 1,200-plus episodes are written by native teachers, annotated, and read aloud. If you go this way, finish the free episodes first and count how many you actually open in a week before you pay.

==Buy the book that matches the level you are at, not the level you wish you were at.==

## Stories you already know

The easiest first story is one you have heard before. You know the plot, so unknown words cannot sink you.

Momotaro, the Peach Boy; Urashima Taro, the fisherman who visits the undersea palace; the Tale of the Bamboo Cutter, Princess Kaguya; and the Grateful Crane. All four are folk tales every Japanese child knows, and all four exist in beginner versions with furigana and audio.

Kaguya and other classics are also free on Aozora Bunko, the public-domain archive of Japanese literature. Read the story you know first, in Japanese, before any new plot. Knowing what happens next is the scaffold that lets you read at all.

## Four things that stop beginners on a Japanese page

**No spaces.** Japanese writes without spaces between words, so you cannot see where one word ends and the next begins. Furigana helps you read the characters, not split them. Expect the first month to feel like reading a wall of characters. It passes with volume.

**One verb, many endings.** 読む becomes 読みます, 読んだ, 読んで, 読めば. They are one word, not five, and your notebook should treat them that way. The next section shows how.

**は and が.** Both look like the subject marker to an English eye, and they do different jobs. Do not memorise rules first. Notice which one the story uses when it introduces someone new, and which one it uses when it talks about someone already there. The pattern shows itself after ten stories.

**One kanji, several readings.** 生 reads せい, しょう, なま, and more, depending on the word it sits in. Furigana tells you the reading in this sentence. The kanji tells you the meaning family. Learn the pair together, never the kanji alone.

## Save the dictionary form, not the ending

Store 読む, not 読みます, 読んだ, and 読んで as three separate words. One entry per verb, with its group and its main forms. That is the whole method. The endings are grammar, and counting them as new words triples your workload for nothing.

Nouns go in with their counter. Store the full phrase 三冊の本. Set phrases go in whole, for example いただきます and お願いします. Keep one notebook across all your books. The same word in three stories is one word learned three times, not three words.

==Store the dictionary form. The endings are grammar, not vocabulary.==

## The ten-minute sampling test

Pick one story and read it once without stopping. A first story runs about 400 to 800 characters. Count the words you cannot read or guess. Fewer than one in twenty, about 95% known, means the level fits. More than that and you are decoding. Drop one level. The story will still be there next month.

---

## Read, then listen, then read again

One story, three passes, three different jobs.

First, read for meaning. Get the whole sentence, skip what you cannot get, and finish the story. Do not stop at every unknown word.

Then listen. Play the audio and follow the words. Spelling meets sound, and the words you only half-read become words you can hear.

Then read for the pattern. Pick the one grammar pattern that repeats in the story, and save one example sentence with it. A story that repeats 〜たい five times has taught you 〜たい. That is the sentence to keep.

Do this with the same story, not three stories. Repetition is where the words stick. For the full method behind this, see [how to learn Japanese by reading](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/learn-japanese-by-reading).

## What not to buy yet

- A second textbook before you finish the first story collection.
- A JLPT prep book before N5. Prep books test, they do not teach reading.
- A "fluent in 30 days" book.
- Only children's picture books. They are too easy after a month, and they teach you words you will never use as an adult.
- The intermediate Richards collection as a first book. See the comparison above.

## The bridge to real Japanese stories

When Tadoku Level 2 feels easy, you are ready for a real story. Start with Akutagawa Ryunosuke's *The Spider's Thread* (蜘蛛の糸, 1918), free on Aozora Bunko. It is short and complete, and famous enough that you already know why it matters. Then try Hoshi Shinichi's short-short stories (ショートショート). Each one runs a few pages and ends with a twist, the classic first real Japanese stories for learners.

Manga is a fair stepping stone too. *Yotsuba&!* (よつばと！), everyday life in plain Japanese, is the usual next step after graded stories. When stories feel easy and you want a whole book, our [Japanese books for beginners](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/japanese-books-for-beginners) post picks the first five by kind.

## FAQ

### Do I need to know kanji before I start?

No. Start with kana and furigana stories at Tadoku Level 0. Kanji comes with the stories. The first collection on this page uses about 250 of them, and each one arrives with furigana and a sentence to meet it in.

### Is romaji OK?

For the first two stories, then drop it. Romaji teaches pronunciation, not reading, and every week on romaji is a week not reading kana.

### How many stories before a novel?

Finish twenty or thirty short stories at your level first, across at least two collections. If you can read a whole story without stopping more than once or twice per page, a short novel is next. If not, the stories are still doing their job.
