# How many words do you need to read a book in a foreign language?

> The honest numbers: about 5,000 word families for rough comprehension, 8,000–9,000 to read comfortably on your own. But the more useful answer is that you don't have to wait until you get there — reading is how you get there.

It's one of the first questions every learner asks, usually while staring at a novel that looks impossibly dense: *how many words do I actually need to know to read this?* It's a good question, and unlike most language-learning questions, it has real numbers behind it.

Here's the honest answer — and then the more useful one.

## The numbers, from actual research

Reading researchers measure this as **lexical coverage**: the percentage of words on a page that you already know. The findings are remarkably consistent:

- **95% coverage** — you know 19 of every 20 words — is roughly the floor for *adequate* comprehension. You'll follow the story, but you'll be guessing often and stopping a lot. That's about **4,000–5,000 word families**.
- **98% coverage** — 49 of every 50 words — is the threshold for *comfortable, independent* reading, where you can enjoy a book without constant lookups. That's about **8,000–9,000 word families**.

A "word family" is a base word plus its obvious relatives (*read, reads, reading, reader*), so it's fewer entries than it sounds. Still: 8,000 families is a lot, and it's why so many learners bounce off their first novel. At 90% coverage — which *feels* like you know a lot — you're missing one word in ten, roughly ten unknown words per page. That's not reading; that's decoding.

## Why the numbers are misleading

Those thresholds describe reading **with no help** — no dictionary, no lookups, just you and the page. That's a real skill and a worthy goal. But it describes the *destination*, not the on-ramp.

Because here's the thing the coverage numbers hide: **the words aren't evenly distributed.** A small number of very common words make up most of any text — the top 2,000 word families alone cover around 80% of a typical novel. The remaining 20% is a long tail of rarer words, most of which show up once or twice. You don't need to *pre-learn* that tail. You need a way to resolve those words cheaply, in context, as you meet them — and each one you meet is one you're now learning.

So the real question isn't "how many words do I need before I can start?" It's "how do I read *now*, at the coverage I have, in a way that closes the gap?"

## How to read below the threshold — and climb it

If you're sitting at 90–95% coverage (where most intermediate learners are), you don't have to grind flashcards up to 8,000 before opening a book. You read, with three habits:

- **Pick books near the top of the frequency curve first.** Children's and young-adult novels use a smaller, more common vocabulary, so your existing coverage goes further. Your first book at 92% coverage is far more pleasant than a literary doorstopper at 92%.
- **Resolve the tail words in place, don't pre-memorise them.** The rare words you hit are exactly the ones worth learning next — and meeting them in a sentence you understand is how they stick. Look them up without leaving the page and keep moving.
- **Let the book build your list.** Every word you stop on is a data point about your personal gap. Collected as you read, those words *are* your study deck — already at your level, already in context.

Do this for a few books and the coverage numbers take care of themselves. You climb from 90% toward 98% not by studying word lists, but by meeting the long tail one novel at a time.
