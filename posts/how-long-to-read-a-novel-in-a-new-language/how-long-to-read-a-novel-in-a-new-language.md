# How long until you can read a novel in a new language?

> The honest answer: sooner than you think if you read the right way, and never if you wait until you feel ready. Here's a realistic timeline — and the one variable that decides it.

It's the question every learner asks eventually, usually while eyeing a real novel on the shelf and quietly deciding they're not good enough yet: *how long until I can actually read this?*

The unsatisfying truth is that there's no date on the calendar. But there is a realistic shape to it, and — more usefully — there's one variable that moves the date forward or back by years. It isn't talent. It's whether you start before you feel ready.

## The rough milestones

These assume you're reading regularly, not cramming grammar and waiting. Ranges are wide because languages and starting points differ, but the shape holds:

- **A2 (a few months in):** children's books and graded readers. Short sentences, tiny recurring vocabulary. *Le Petit Prince*, *Heidi*, a first Grimm tale. You'll lean on lookups, and that's the point.
- **B1 (roughly six months to a year of steady reading):** your first real short novel — a Kafka novella, a Mérimée, a Keller. Slow, with help, but genuinely reading adult prose. This is the milestone most people mean when they say "read a novel," and it's closer than they think.
- **B2 (a year or two in):** full-length novels at a comfortable pace, looking up only the words that block meaning. Reading starts to feel like reading.
- **C1+ (a few years, if you keep going):** the hard stuff — Mann, Proust — unfolds on the first pass. You've stopped counting.

Notice these are measured in *books read*, not months enrolled. Someone who reads five short books in six months is far ahead of someone who's "studied for two years" and never opened one.

## The variable that actually decides it

Two learners at the same level, same motivation. One can read a novel a year from now; the other still can't in three. The difference is almost always **reading volume**, and reading volume is almost always decided by **friction**.

Here's the mechanism. Every unknown word is a small decision: stop and look it up, or push through. If looking up a word is expensive — reach for a dictionary, type it, lose your place, come back — you'll do one of two bad things: stop reading (too tiring) or stop looking things up (so you understand less and enjoy it less, then stop reading anyway). Either way the volume collapses, and with it the timeline stretches out for years.

Drop the cost of a lookup to almost nothing and the whole equation changes. You look up the words that matter, ignore the ones that don't, keep the plot moving, and — crucially — you keep reading tomorrow. Low friction is what turns "I studied for two years" into "I've read fifteen books."

## So: start now, one level too easy

The move is not to wait until some future readiness. It's to start today with a book one notch below where your pride wants to be, read five pages a night, and look up only what blocks you. The readiness you're waiting for is *manufactured by doing this*, not before it.
