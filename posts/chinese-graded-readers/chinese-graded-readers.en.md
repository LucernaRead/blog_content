Chinese asks more of a beginner than most languages do. There is no alphabet to lean on, every new word is also a drawing to memorise, and a text that looks short on the page can still be dense with characters you have never seen.

Graded readers are the controlled way in: books written or adapted for learners, with the character and word range held to a stated level, so your first book in Chinese can be a complete story instead of a page you abandon.

This guide explains how the Chinese level systems work, which series sit where, and how to test a book before you buy it. Edition details were checked against publisher and bookseller pages, and the titles are starting points.

---

## What counts as a Chinese graded reader?

A Chinese graded reader is a text written or adapted for learners in which the vocabulary range, character range and grammar are held to a level the publisher states. Support usually travels with it: a glossary, pinyin for new words, English translations, comprehension questions, or audio.

Two near neighbours cause most of the confusion. A book for Chinese schoolchildren assumes a native child's character knowledge, which grows in a different order from an adult learner's HSK list. And a textbook passage stops when its grammar point is done. A graded reader keeps going until the story ends, and that length is where the repetition does its work.

Chinese has a deeper graded shelf than most languages. Finding a book is easy. The difficulty is that the levels on the covers are measured in three different units.

---

## Three ways levels are counted, and they do not convert

Open three Chinese readers side by side and you will find three different numbers, all calling themselves the level:

- **HSK vocabulary.** The six-level HSK scale most current books still use counts cumulative words: HSK 1 is 150 words, HSK 2 is 300, HSK 3 is 600, HSK 4 is 1,200, HSK 5 is 2,500 and HSK 6 is 5,000 or more. A newer standard published in 2021 redraws the map into nine bands with much larger lists, so check which system a publisher means before you trust an HSK label on a recent book.
- **Unique characters.** Mandarin Companion grades by characters: Breakthrough uses 150 unique characters, Level 1 uses 300 and Level 2 uses 450. A 300-character book and a 300-word book are different objects, because characters combine to form many words.
- **Base words.** Chinese Breeze grades by base words, from 300 at Level 1 to 1,100 at Level 4 in the publisher's current listing, with the series preface planning eight levels reaching 4,500 words.

None of these numbers converts cleanly into another. Treat each label as an address inside one series only, pick a first sample with it, then let the sample decide. ## The ladder: five starting points

| Starting point | Level as stated | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| Mandarin Companion | 150 / 300 / 450 unique characters | Short novels, mostly adapted classics, in simplified and traditional editions |
| Chinese Breeze (Cheng & Tsui) | 300 to 1,100 base words, Levels 1 to 4 | Original stories across genres, with exercises and streamed audio |
| Rainbow Bridge (Sinolingua) | 150 to 2,500 words, Starter to Level 6 | Chinese myths, legends and history, with an English translation in the book |
| Graded Readers for Chinese Language Learners (BLCUP) | Levels 1 to 3 | Folktales and the four great classical novels, retold at length |
| Imagin8 Press, *Journey to the West* | From a 600-word vocabulary, rising to 2,000 | One long classic in 31 short books, pinyin facing the text |

**The first book.** Mandarin Companion is where most beginners start, because its floor is the lowest on this list. The Breakthrough level tells complete stories with 150 unique characters: original tales such as *My Teacher Is a Martian* and *Just Friends?* sit alongside the Zhou Haisheng stories. Level 1, at 300 characters, moves to adapted classics, including *The Secret Garden*, *Emma* and *The Prince and the Pauper* (Level 1, ISBN 9781941875223). Characters above the level are underlined where they appear and glossed at the foot of the page. Most titles exist in both simplified and traditional editions.

**The long ladder.** Chinese Breeze, published by Cheng & Tsui, climbs further with original stories written for learners: detective stories, romance, fantasy and horror. The publisher's current listing runs from Level 1 (300 base words) to Level 4 (1,100 base words), and the books grow from about 8,000 to 30,000 characters in length. Each book ends with a vocabulary list and exercises, and audio can be streamed by scanning the QR code on the back cover.

**The Chinese-story shelf.** Sinolingua's Rainbow Bridge series grades by vocabulary and states its HSK and CEFR mapping on the cover: Starter is 150 words (HSK 1, A1), then 300, 500, 750 and 1,000 words, Level 5 is 1,500 words (HSK 4), and Level 6 is 2,500 words (HSK 5, B2 to C1). The stories are mythology, legends, folklore and history, so the vocabulary leans towards kings, armies and ancient China. Newer books add a full English translation, and audio is available through the publisher.

**The classical bridge.** Beijing Language and Culture University Press publishes *Graded Readers for Chinese Language Learners*, a set of 50 books in three subseries (folktales, literary stories and historical stories) at Levels 1 to 3. The literary subseries has 20 books drawn from the four great classical novels, each running to 20,000 to 30,000 characters. Its *Journey to the West* volume 2 is a Level 2 book at an 800-word level (ISBN 9787561943410, 104 pages). This is the shelf to climb once a 300-character novel feels short.

**One classic, start to finish.** Imagin8 Press tells *Journey to the West* again, in a separate series by Jeff Pepper and Xiao Hui Wang. It runs to 31 individual books, starting at a 600-word vocabulary (the HSK 3 range) and rising to 2,000 words, with pinyin facing each page of Chinese, a full English translation, a glossary and free audio. The same names, places and verbs recur for months, until they cost nothing to recognise.

## Simplified or traditional, pinyin or none

Chinese adds two choices no European language asks for. Decide both before you buy, because they change what a book trains.

Script comes first. Learn the script of the place and people you read for: simplified for mainland China and Singapore, traditional for Taiwan, Hong Kong and much of the overseas shelf. Mandarin Companion's dual editions make this easy at the bottom of the ladder. Higher up, many Chinese-published series are simplified only, a real constraint for traditional-script learners, so check the edition page.

Pinyin comes second. Beginners often want pinyin over every line, and most graded readers refuse: your eyes take the easier path and the characters stop registering. The common compromise is characters as the main text, with pinyin reserved for the glossary and for new words. Imagin8's facing pinyin is the exception, and it suits readers whose character knowledge lags far behind their listening.

## A ten-minute sample test

Labels narrow the choice. A sample settles it. Read the first pages of a candidate book for ten minutes: can you follow who did what, are unknown characters occasional rather than constant, and do you want the next page at the end?

If you meet an unknown word every line or two, the book is above your level for now, whatever its cover says. One unknown word every page or so, most of them guessable from context, means the level fits. If a whole chapter passes with nothing new, take the next rung up, or read this one quickly for volume and speed. Publishers post samples freely at the bottom of the ladder, so the test costs nothing.

Paper graded readers are cheap, light and easy to reread. Ebooks add the feature Chinese reading wants most: tap a character and get its reading and meaning at once.

## When should you try an original?

After the classical bridge, the gap to real Chinese narrows but does not close. Children's novels and web fiction are the usual next step, and a story you already know in translation helps for the reason [rereading a book you already know](https://lucernaread.com/landing/blog/reread-a-book-you-know-foreign-language) sets out: the plot carries you while character recognition is still slow. Expect names and descriptive passages to stay hard longest.

## Questions before you choose

### Which HSK level should my first graded reader be?

Start one step below the HSK level you are studying. A reader at your study level leaves no room to build speed. For most learners that means a 150 or 300 character or word book first.

### Is a 300-character book the same as a 300-word book?

No. Mandarin Companion's 300 counts unique characters, and Chinese Breeze's 300 counts base words. Characters combine into words, so the two 300s describe different amounts of language. Compare inside one series, and use a sample across series.

### Should I learn simplified or traditional characters?

Learn the script used where you will read: simplified for mainland China, traditional for Taiwan and Hong Kong. Several beginner series publish both editions, so the choice costs nothing early. It gets harder to switch later.
